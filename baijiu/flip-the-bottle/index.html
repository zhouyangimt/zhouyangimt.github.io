Flip the Bottle: How to Buy Baijiu Without Getting Robbed

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-09-27 baijiu buying-guide light-aroma strong-aroma gift

The man behind the counter never looks at the price. He turns the bottle over in one hand, tilts the back label toward the light coming through the doorway, and reads it the way a radiologist reads a film. Then he sets it down and hands me a different bottle. Twenty yuan cheaper. No gift box. The label half worn off.

I have been buying from this shop on a side street off Huaihai Road for nine years. The bottle he rejected had a famous name on the front and a number on the back he did not like. The one he chose is plain glass with a paper label and a price a student could pay. He said five words, kan houmian de biaozhun hao, look at the standard number on the back, and went back to his newspaper.

That number is the whole buying guide. Every legal bottle of baijiu in China carries it: a national standard, four digits, printed somewhere on the back of the label. It answers one question, and at the cheap end of the shelf it is the only question worth asking. Did this spirit get fermented out of grain, or mixed in a tank from industrial alcohol and flavoring?

The friend standing next to me that afternoon, a British architect with 300 yuan in his hand and no Mandarin, was one bottle away from getting it wrong. Chinese shoppers flip the bottle before they look at the price tag. This is how you do it too.

Four Digits, One Question

Solid-state fermentation, guti fa, is the process the entire culture rests on. Grain is steamed, mixed with a brick of moldy wheat or sorghum called daqu, packed into stone pits or clay jars, and left alone for weeks or months while mold, yeast and bacteria work through it. The alcohol comes out of that pile. Nothing is added afterwards except time. If you want the long version of that process, it is in Baijiu 101.

Bottles made this way get stamped by aroma type, so the standard number doubles as a tasting note. 10781.1 is strong aroma, the sweet pineapple-and-wet-cellar style. 10781.2 is light aroma, clean and dry. 10781.3 is rice aroma, soft and faintly floral. 26760 is sauce aroma, the soy sauce and mushroom family that made Moutai famous. A dozen smaller numbers cover regional styles.

The two numbers you find on the bottom shelf tell the opposite story. 20822 means solid-liquid: part of the alcohol came from fermented grain, the rest was distilled alcohol blended in. 20821 means liquid-state: food-grade ethanol, water, and xiangjing, the aromatic compounds that make it smell like the real thing. Both are legal. Both sell by the truckload. Neither is worth paying aged-money for, and neither will ever improve in a glass left on the table overnight.

I spent 13 years reading traces for a living, and the habit transfers cleanly. The front label is a marketing page. The standard number is the artifact. When the fine print says 20821, believe the fine print.

Cheap 20821 is not obviously poison, which is the trap. It goes down thin and slightly sweet, burns at the front of the tongue and then quits, and by midnight the empty glass smells like nothing at all. A 50-yuan bottle of solid-state light aroma is a different animal: dry, clean, a little like steamed sorghum, warm going down and gone quickly, with no headache waiting in the morning. Same alcohol percentage on both labels. Completely different drink.

The Box Is for the Guest

Liquor shopping in China runs on a second rule that has nothing to do with chemistry: everyone at the table already knows the price. Western wine can hide behind a region and a vintage. A bottle of Wuliangye on a Chinese table announces exactly what the host thinks of you, in a number all the guests can read without asking. Gifting is where that weight lives, and it comes with real risk attached, which is the part the Moutai story makes plain.

So the same distillery sells the same liquid twice. Once in bare glass, once in a lacquered box with a magnetic lid and a ribbon. The bare bottle has a tradition of its own: guangping jiu, light-bottle liquor. Erguotou, literally second-pot liquor, is named for the middle of the distillation run. The first and last cuts are set aside and only the second pot goes into the bottle. In Beijing a 20-yuan bare glass bottle sits on the same table as the food, and no one apologises for it.

The rule that falls out of this is simple. The box is for the guest. The liquid is for you. Buy the box only when someone else is going to open it.

How to Buy It Without Getting Robbed

Flip it. One of the 10781 numbers, 26760, or a regional standard. Put 20821 and 20822 back on the shelf unless the bottle costs less than a bowl of noodles and you are cooking with it.

What the money actually buys. Below about 60 yuan lives the everyday shelf: Fenjiu's bare-bottle light aroma, Yanghe's cheapest, Jianzhuang. From 100 to 300 yuan, the everyday bottle turns into a guest bottle. Old Baifen in the light-aroma family, Tequ on the strong-aroma side, Xijiu and Moutai Prince in sauce. My own pick is Old Baifen, around 150 yuan the last time I looked, and it keeps embarrassing bottles three times its price. There is no 900-yuan liquid hiding in a 100-yuan bottle. There is a 400-yuan liquid hiding in a 150-yuan bottle, and it is worth learning which one it is.

Abroad, three places work. The Chinese supermarket, wherever you live: 99 Ranch, H Mart, T&T, SeeWoo in London, and their equivalents in most large cities. Wine.com and Total Wine ship spirits in many US states and keep a real baijiu shelf. Duty-free on the way out of Beijing, Shanghai or Hong Kong is the third. Check the standard number at all three. Export bottlings sometimes print it in small English text, and the number is still there.

Never buy a famous name from a marketplace seller. Moutai is among the most counterfeited spirits on earth, the fakes are good enough to fool an experienced shopkeeper, and a marketplace listing or a WeChat contact who can get it cheap is the single most common way foreigners get robbed in this category. Buy from a chain, a distillery store or an airport counter, and keep the receipt.

Do not buy age. A 90-yuan bottle stamped 30-year reserve is ninety yuan of liquor with a nice word printed on it. Real aged base is scarce, and it is not sold at that price in a 500ml bottle. Judge the liquid, not the number in the name.
