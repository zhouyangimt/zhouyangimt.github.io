Jade Ice Burn: The Baijiu That Ages With Pork Fat

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-10-17 baijiuchi-aromacantonesefermentationorigin-story

The shed in Shiwan is quiet in the way a working building rarely is. Half-ton earthenware jars stand in rows, each mouth tied over with white cloth and capped with a disc of clay. A worker walks the aisle, unties one, lifts the lid, and the smell comes out slowly: steamed rice first, then something saline and savoury, closer to a crock of fermented black beans than to any liquor I know. He dips a steel ladle and brings up a glass of absolutely clear spirit. A slab of pork fat turns over in it, soft as a piece of old jade, and settles back down.

This is 玉冰烧, juk bing siu in Cantonese, "jade ice burn." It is the only baijiu in China whose national standard requires meat in the jar. It is also, at 29 percent alcohol, roughly half the strength of the stuff foreigners gag on at banquets, and it is drunk at eight in the morning with dumplings. Both of those facts are the same fact, and this is the story of why.

The Rice Comes Before the Pork

Foshan sits in the Pearl River Delta, an hour south of Guangzhou, and its liquor has nothing in common with the sorghum spirits of the north. The grain here is rice, all of it. The starter is not the big wheat brick that Moutai leans on but 小曲, "small qu": rice flour mixed with herbs and a local mold culture, rolled into balls the size of a marble and left to bloom. Small qu works fast and hot, and it makes a spirit that smells of flowers and steamed grain rather than soy sauce and mushrooms.

Rice is steamed, spread to cool, mixed with qu and water, and left in open vats for two weeks or so, half solid, half liquid, fermenting without any attempt to keep the air out. Then it goes into a pot still. What comes off is already only about 30 percent alcohol, and at that point it has a name: 斋酒, zhai jiu, "vegetarian liquor." It has not met the pork yet. The distillers use the word with a completely straight face, which I find funnier than they do.

The vegetarian spirit goes into a 埕, the big glazed jar, and this is where the town matters. Shiwan is a pottery town before it is a liquor town. The Nanfeng kiln on the edge of the district has been firing since the Ming dynasty, and the same clay that made teapots and roof tiles made the jars. A Shiwan jar is not packaging. It is slightly porous, it breathes, and its rough interior gives the spirit something to push against for the next few years.

What the Fat Actually Does

Now the pork. Back fat, steamed to firm it up and clean it, cut into slabs and lowered into the jar before the lid goes on. A few kilos to the ton, depending on the house. It sits there for months, sometimes years, and it does two jobs that people usually collapse into one.

The first is scavenging. New rice spirit is sharp: fusel alcohols, aldehydes, a sulphur edge that catches at the back of your throat. Those compounds are fat-soluble and alcohol-soluble; the fat is not. So they migrate out of the spirit and into the fat, the way a bowl of fat in a fridge pulls the smell out of the air. The spirit comes out rounder, softer, and it loses that nail-polish sting without losing its aroma.

The second job is building something new. Fat is not inert in a jar of 30 percent alcohol. Its fatty acids slowly break down into dicarboxylic acids, and those esterify into compounds like diethyl suberate and diethyl azelate. That is where 豉香 comes from, the "soy-sauce aroma" the style is named for: a saline, savoury, faintly fermented-bean note that no other baijiu has. I spent thirteen years at Microsoft, Google and NVIDIA watching background jobs do maintenance on live data, and this is the closest thing I have met in a jar. The fat garbage-collects the off-notes and writes a new field into the record at the same time. And unlike every filter I have ever maintained, this one works better the longer it stays in service.

What it tastes like, finally, is not meat. The nose is rice pudding and salted plum, with that beancurd-skin savouriness underneath. The body is thin, almost watery, with a slight oiliness on the tongue, sweet at the front and saline at the finish. At 29 percent there is no burn at all. If you have trained yourself to brace before baijiu, this is the bottle that makes the training useless.

Why It Is Only 29 Percent

You can make baijiu at any strength from 18 to 68, and this style sits at the bottom on purpose. Partly it is the still: a rice mash fermented half-solid gives a low-proof distillate, and nobody in Foshan saw a reason to dilute it further or rectify it up. Partly it is the fat, which would rather work in a gentler solution than in 50 percent ethanol. Mostly it is the table. This was built as a morning drink for a city that eats breakfast for two hours, and a 53 percent spirit is not something you sip through a second pot of tea.

The history is short, as these things go. A distillery called 陈太吉 opened in Shiwan in 1830, and in the 1890s a Hanlin scholar named Chen Ruyue came home to run it. The story credits him with the fat, and with the name: the liquor was first sold as 肉冰烧, "meat ice burn," until somebody pointed out that the word for meat and the word for jade are the same sound in Cantonese. Both are juk. One character sells a drink with breakfast; the other sells a drink with a story. The label has read 玉冰烧 ever since.

How to Drink It Without Ruining It

Buy by the standard, not the brand. The back label should say 豉香型白酒 and carry GB/T 16289, the national standard for the style; that is the only guarantee the pork was ever involved. Shiwan Yubingshao is the benchmark and costs almost nothing — twenty to sixty yuan for a bottle, which is part of the point. The 45 percent version exists and is a different, hotter drink; start at 29.

Serve it at cellar temperature, 16 to 18°C, in the small lipped glass the Chinese use for everything. No ice, which flattens the savoury edge, and no warming, which drives off the esters you paid for. Then eat: roast goose, white-cut chicken, spare ribs steamed in black bean, a bowl of congee, salted peanuts. The liquor is thin and saline and it was designed to cut through fat, so give it some fat to cut.

Keep the bottle upright, in the dark, and finish it within a year of opening. At 29 percent there is not enough alcohol to hold the line against oxygen, and a half-empty bottle left on a shelf through a summer goes flat and sour. Do not judge it next to a 53 percent sauce-aroma spirit either. It is not competing in that category. It is a table wine with a spirit's backbone, and the correct comparison is a dry sherry, not Moutai.
