The Pit That Never Stopped: 450 Years of Mud, and the Men Who Tend It

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-09-20 baijiustrong-aromaluzhoucraft-heritagefermentation

The mud is not one color. In the low light of the pit hall it reads brown-black, but shift your angle and patches of it flash green, then red, then a flat mineral blue. Four and a half centuries of fermentation did that. The yellow clay dug from Wudu Creek in 1573 has been soaked, dried and soaked again so many times that it has stopped being yellow at all.

I am on a stone walkway in Luzhou, southern Sichuan, with fermentation pits on both sides. Each one is capped under a dome of wet clay, smoothed flat by hand, still cold and faintly tacky under a palm. The air is warm and sour-sweet, part bakery, part barn, with alcohol underneath it. Below those clay domes, sorghum has been sitting in the dark for sixty days, doing the slow anaerobic work that turns starch into what the Chinese call 浓香 (nong xiang) — strong aroma.

Four of these pits have not been empty since the Ming dynasty.

The Mud Is the Recipe

A strong-aroma pit is not a container. It is a farm, and the crop is bacteria.

The pit is a rectangle dug a little over two meters into the floor and lined with clay. Steamed sorghum goes in with qu (曲), the starter brick pressed from wheat or barley and left out in a warm room to be colonized by wild mold and yeast. What matters is what stays behind when the mash comes out. The grain leaves; the microbes do not. They move into the pit walls, a few centimeters deep, and they survive there between batches, feeding on what the last batch left behind. By the time a pit has been working for a century, those walls hold a microbial community that has been selecting itself the entire time.

Sequencing studies keep finding a crowded neighborhood in aged pit mud: Clostridium and Bacillus, lactic-acid bacteria, methane-producing archaea that have no business living in a liquor pit, and the caproic-acid producers that matter most. Caproic acid meeting ethanol is what makes ethyl hexanoate, the pineapple-and-banana note that separates strong aroma from every other style. New pits have thinner mud, fewer species, and flatter liquor. Nobody has found a shortcut, and the industry has been looking for one since the 1950s.

The way I think about it is state. A light-aroma distillery in Shanxi ferments in ceramic jars it can empty and reset. That is a stateless container, and its output is clean and repeatable. A Luzhou pit is a machine nobody has rebooted since 1573. Its whole value is accumulated state, and the state is alive, which means it dies if the pit dries out or goes cold. There is no restore from backup. You can buy the same yellow clay, mix the same qu, steam the same sorghum, and you will pour a different drink.

The distillers have a saying for it: 千年老窖万年糟. A thousand-year pit, a ten-thousand-year mash.

Three Names, One Mud

The first pit was dug by a soldier. Shu Chengzong had passed the imperial military exam, come home to Luzhou, and in 1573 built a workshop he called Shujuyuan beside a well whose water he liked. His name is on the method as it was first written down: grain layered into the pit, solid-state fermentation, the mud itself as the source of aroma, old mash fed back into new grain so the cycle never fully breaks. He was not a chemist. He was a man who noticed what worked, and his pits are still producing.

The twentieth century asked more of that inheritance. Lei Gaohuai was born in 1934 into a Luzhou distilling family rich enough that the city called them half of it. After 1949 the family workshops became a state factory and the wine master's son became a pit laborer, carrying three-hundred-jin loads on his shoulders at twenty years old. He spent the next sixty years on a stranger project: learning how to grow pit mud from scratch. He published the first textbook on strong-aroma production in 1959, won a provincial science prize in 1979 for artificially cultivating cellar mud, and in 1988 put blending formulas on a computer, which was an exotic thing to do to a liquor back then. He died in 2023, at eighty-nine, still listed as a national inheritor of the technique.

Three men have held that title for Luzhou's method since 2007. In my last piece I wrote that the pit has been tended down nineteen generations. The count of hands is honestly fuzzy. The mud is not. What a master still carries in his own hands is the part no instrument covers: how the clay takes a thumbprint on the day the pit is sealed, how the mash smells when the seal comes off, and whether fermentation ran slow at the start, firm in the middle and slow at the end. The distillers have a phrase for that curve, 前缓、中挺、后缓落, and somebody has to feel it every day for sixty days.

How to Get Close to It

You cannot drink four hundred and fifty years out of a bottle. You can stand in front of it. The four Ming pits sit inside the working Luzhou Laojiao plant on Sanxing Street in Jiangyang district, listed as national cultural relics in 1996, free to visit but only by appointment, surrounded by equipment that is still in use. It is not a museum that happens to make liquor. It is a factory that happens to be protected.

For the bottle, read two things. First, 固态发酵, solid-state fermentation, on the back label. If the ingredients list 食用酒精 or 液态法 instead, you are holding grain alcohol with flavoring added, and the mud had nothing to do with it. Second, 窖龄, pit age, which strong-aroma houses now print on premium bottles. Pit age is real and it is also a marketing instrument: thirty, sixty and ninety-year pits all exist, but the price gaps between them are wider than the flavor gaps. What the blender does on top of those pits matters more than most labels admit.

The cheapest honest entry is the same company's basic 特曲, drunk at room temperature with cold sliced pork and peanuts. You will taste the pineapple note, and you will understand, without any of the above, why a family would keep a hole in the ground alive for four hundred years.
