Three Glasses, One Table: How to Actually Drink Baijiu

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-09-26 baijiutastingsauce-aromastrong-aromapairing

Three bottles stand on my kitchen table in Shanghai, all still capped, and I know which one is which without looking. It is a Tuesday. This is not a party. It is homework: one Moutai, one Wuliangye, one Guojiao 1573, three small glasses, and a dish of peanuts going soft because I keep forgetting to eat them.

I do this maybe twice a year. Not to get drunk, because 53 percent alcohol settles that question quickly, but because baijiu is the only spirit I know whose taste changes depending on what is on the plate next to it. You can drink Scotch alone in an armchair. Baijiu wants company, and food.

Pour an inch into each glass. Moutai first. Lift it to your chin, not your nose. What comes up is not smoke or vanilla or fruit. It is soy sauce, wet bread, and something faintly medicinal, like the inside of a Chinese pharmacy. All of that happens before you taste anything. This is what trips up every foreigner I hand a glass to: baijiu is judged by how it smells first and how it tastes second, backwards from everything the West taught you about liquor.

Three Stages, in Order

Chinese tasters read a glass in three moves and have had names for them for centuries: 闻香 (wen xiang), the smell; 入口 (ru kou), the entry; 回味 (hui wei), the returning flavor, what the mouth is still doing forty seconds later. The last one carries the most information. Anyone can catch the soy sauce. The question is what it leaves behind.

The aroma itself is not a mystery, just a big one. It comes from 大曲 (daqu), a brick of wheat that people deliberately infect with mold, yeast and bacteria, then dry in a warm room. The qu used for sauce-aroma baijiu is dried at 60 degrees Celsius or higher, which kills most of the microbes and leaves a narrow set of heat-loving survivors that grow in commercial quantity in only a few river valleys. That is why 酱香 smells like fermented soybean paste and soy sauce: the bacteria making it are the same family that make those. The molecule behind the smoky, clove-like edge of a good Moutai, 4-ethylguaiacol, is the same one that gives bacon its smell.

Two tests are worth stealing from a Chinese liquor shop. First, 挂杯, the legs: tilt the glass and watch what runs down the side. Thick, slow, oily streaks mean long-chain esters, which correlate loosely with age. Second, 空杯留香, the empty cup. Finish a glass of real sauce-aroma baijiu and leave it on the table overnight. Next morning that glass still smells like soy sauce. A cheap bottle's glass smells like nothing by midnight, because the compounds that carry the aroma were never in it.

Then there is the burn. Fifty-three percent alcohol is going to hurt somewhere, and the only real question is where. In a rough bottle the heat hits the front of the tongue and spreads like a slap, then quits. In an aged one it sits farther back, softer, and it resolves into sweetness instead of fading out. Acetaldehyde and fusel alcohols are what your mouth is registering, not the ethanol; slow filtering and years in ceramic convert enough of them to change the shape of the sensation.

I spent 13 years staring at profiler traces, and a tasting note is the same job. You are not reading the spike, you are reading the tail: two baijiu at 52 percent can peak identically and part ways completely over the next forty seconds.

Same table, three glasses

Moutai (酱香, sauce aroma, 53%). Nose: soy sauce, fermented bean, a little floral perfume, a whiff of solvent at the very top. Entry: soft and oddly oily for 53 percent, sweet before the alcohol arrives. Mid-palate: mushroom broth, actual umami, closer to food than to drink. At the back, a flash of ripe pineapple. Finish: long, bitter at first and then sweet, and it does not stop. The empty glass smells like soy sauce in the morning.

Wuliangye (浓香, strong aroma, 52%). Five grains instead of one, fermented in mud pits. Nose: sweeter, more floral, pineapple and honey, less medicine. Entry: rounder, juicier, easier. This is the bottle to pour for the friend who swears they hate Chinese liquor. Finish: shorter and cleaner than Moutai's, warm, pleasant, gone.

Guojiao 1573 (浓香, 52%, from pits dug in 1573 and never scrubbed since). Nose: quieter than either of the others, wet earth, ink, cooked mushroom, a trace of barnyard. Palate: 陈香, the aged note, which lands as a bitterness that reads expensive rather than flawed. Finish: longer and drier than Wuliangye's, and plainly older. If Wuliangye is the extrovert of strong aroma, this one has been reading.

What baijiu smells like, in one line: a wet cellar in autumn, soy sauce, ripe pineapple, nail polish remover, and the sweet edge of fermenting rice. Roughly that order. Never twice at the same strength.

Why the Table Matters More Than the Glass

Distilled spirits became the everyday strong drink of China gradually, from the Ming dynasty on, and 白酒 itself is a 20th-century word; before that people said 烧酒, burnt liquor. Two of the drink's most common words point at the table. 品酒, to taste, is done sitting down, quietly, with food. 干杯, to dry the cup, is done standing up, usually in a room with too many people in it.

干杯 has no interest in whether you like the taste. At a Chinese banquet the host proposes the first three toasts and everyone empties all three; the next round belongs to you. The ritual is a receipt. The glass is empty, therefore the respect was real. Toasting with both hands and the rim of your glass below an elder's is the physical grammar of it. The real social skill is not holding your liquor. It is working out early which register the table is in.

How Not to Ruin It

Room temperature, never ice. Cold shuts down the esters and turns the texture greasy in the mouth. Pour 15 to 25 millilitres, a small cup rather than a shot glass.

Nose from below the rim, not into it. Bury your nose in a 53 percent glass once and your receptors stop reporting for a full minute.

Eat fat and salt with it. Braised pork, cold sliced beef, peanuts, Sichuan food with chili oil, anything out of a hotpot. Wine pairing is about balance; this is the opposite. Baijiu is built to stand up to fat and heat. Skip dessert, and skip sushi.

Sip the good stuff. If you would rather not, that is fine. 随意 (sui yi), as you please, and 以茶代酒 (yi cha dai jiu), tea instead of liquor, are both legitimate at any Chinese table, and nobody pushes past either one.

Learn on the cheap bottle of the right process. A 100-yuan 汾酒, light aroma, clean and dry, will teach you the aroma system without gift-bottle money. Find out whether you like baijiu before you spend like you already do.
