# Roast Goose: The Skin Is the Point

The geese hang in a row behind the glass, and they glow. Not shine — glow. Each bird the color of dark amber, lacquered to a mirror, the skin drawn tight as a drumhead over all that fat underneath. A heat lamp beats down on them, and the shop smells of five-spice and charred maltose and the faint metallic sweetness of hot fat. You don't read a menu. You point at the biggest one and hold up two fingers.

The man behind the counter takes it down and lays it on a chopping block the size of a tree stump. He doesn't ask if you want it boneless, doesn't offer a fork. He raises the cleaver and brings it down — thwack — through the spine, through the ribs, clean to the board. Thwack. Thwack. Thwack. The leg first, then the breast in five even strips, then the wing. He scrapes the blade across the board and the juices that had been hiding inside the bird spill out over the pieces, dark and glossy, faintly sweet. Then he spoons the plum sauce into a tiny dish and slides the whole thing across the counter.

This is roast goose — 深井烧鹅 (*shēnjǐng shāo'é*), the full name, after the village of Sham Tseng in Hong Kong where the best of it comes from. If you've only ever eaten roast duck, you've been eating the junior varsity.

The difference is the skin. Peking duck skin is air and crackle, paper-thin, made to be lifted off and wrapped in a pancake. Roast goose skin is thicker — not flabby thick, but armor thick, the kind that keeps its own snap after it's been chopped and sauced and sitting under the lamp for an hour. When you bite it, it doesn't just crackle. It shatters, then gives way to a layer of fat so rich it coats the roof of your mouth, and then the meat underneath, dark and dense, closer to beef than to chicken. Goose is to duck what duck is to chicken. Wilder. Deeper. A bird that actually worked for a living.

The magic happens in the days before the fire. The bird is marinated with a paste of fermented bean curd, five-spice, sugar, and rice wine, then the skin is brushed with a glaze of maltose and vinegar and hung to dry until it's tight and tacky. The maltose caramelizes into that amber glass; the vinegar keeps it from burning. Roast it too fast and the skin blisters. Roast it right — in a deep charcoal oven, the bird hanging and turning, basted in its own drippings — and you get skin that snaps like toffee.

Then there's the sauce, and this is where most bad versions fail. The real one is plum sauce — 酸梅酱 (*suānméi jiàng*) — sour-sweet, the color of rust, sharp enough to cut through all that fat. It is not sweet-and-sour sauce from a jar. If your goose arrives with a neon orange dip that tastes like candy, you are eating a lie, and you should say so out loud.

You'll know a bad goose the moment it lands. Flabby, pale skin that tears instead of shattering means it was steamed, or roasted and then refrigerated and microwaved back to life — the fat rendered out, the skin gone limp as a wet sock. That's not a roast. That's a crime scene. And if the meat comes out dry and stringy, the shop has been holding the bird too long under the lamp, waiting for someone who will settle. Don't settle.

Eat the leg first, while it's hot. That's the rule. The leg is where the fat lives and the skin is thickest, where the meat comes off the bone in dark, fibrous ropes. Tear it apart with chopsticks — a knife and fork will only slow you down — and drag each piece through the plum sauce, cool and sharp against the hot fat. Then take a bite of plain rice between mouthfuls. The rice isn't filler. It's the resting place for the juices, and by the end of the bowl it'll be stained brown and taste better than anything else on the table.

I grew up on roast duck, not goose. In Shanghai, the roast duck of my childhood was a Beijing-style affair — sliced at the table, wrapped in pancakes, a ceremony more than a meal. Roast goose doesn't do ceremony. There's a cleaver and a chopping block and a man who's done the same three thwacks ten thousand times. The first time I ate it properly was in a shop the size of a phone booth in Shenzhen, standing at the counter because there were no seats, sauce on my fingers, watching the geese glow in the window. I've been ruined for roast duck ever since.

That's the thing about roast goose. It doesn't perform. It just hangs there behind the glass, glowing amber, fat dripping slow as honey, waiting for you to point. And when you do, the cleaver comes down — thwack — and dinner is decided.
