The Chinese Steamer: Water Does the Work

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-08-10 steamerbamboo-steamersteamingkitchen-toolsequipment

Why Steam?

Most Western home cooks own a steamer basket they've used exactly twice. It lives in the back of the pot drawer, tangled in a forgotten colander. In China, steaming (蒸, zhēng) isn't a health-food afterthought — it's a primary cooking method. Walk through any morning market in Guangzhou and you'll count more steamers than woks. The reason is simple: steaming does what frying and boiling can't. It cooks food in its own juices without adding fat. It preserves texture. It's gentle enough for a whole fish and fast enough for dumplings. And unlike a wok, which demands your attention, a steamer mostly just sits there doing its job while you prep the next dish.

I learned this the hard way. My first attempt at steamed fish involved a wok, an overturned bowl, and a ceramic plate balanced on top — the Chinese equivalent of cooking over a campfire. The plate slipped. The fish landed in the water. The broth became fish tea. Buy a proper steamer.

Bamboo vs Metal

Bamboo steamers (蒸笼, zhēnglóng): The traditional choice. The woven bamboo lid absorbs condensation instead of dripping it back onto your food — so dumplings stay dry on top, not soggy. They look great, they smell like bamboo tea when hot, and they stack. Downside: they stain, they can mold if not dried properly, and they need a wok or wide pot to sit on. Metal steamers: practical and indestructible. Stainless steel, dishwasher-safe, no mold concerns. But the flat metal lid drips condensed water back down, which can make dumpling tops wet and bao buns blotchy. The Japanese-style metal steamers with domed lids (like a donabe steamer) partially solve this.

My take: bamboo for dumplings and dim sum, metal for vegetables and fish. If you're only getting one, get bamboo and learn to dry it properly.

Size & Stacking

For a home kitchen: 10-12 inches (25-30cm) diameter. Two tiers is the sweet spot — enough for a whole fish or two dozen dumplings. Three tiers lets you cook a full meal in one go (fish on top, vegetables in the middle, rice at the bottom) but requires a burner with enough BTUs to push steam through all three layers. The steamer sits on or in a pot/wok of boiling water — you need about 2-3 inches of water depth, replenished every 15-20 minutes for long cooks.

Get a steamer with a snug-fitting lid. Steam escaping around the edges means water evaporating faster than it should.

How to Use It

Water goes in, water boils, steam rises. Sounds simple. The details: Water already boiling BEFORE you put the steamer on. You want instant steam, not a gradual warmup that makes dumplings stick to their liners. Line the steamer floor. Bamboo: perforated parchment paper, cabbage leaves, or reusable silicone liners. Metal: parchment or a light oil rub. Never let food touch bare metal or bamboo directly — it'll stick and tear.

Lid stays ON. Every time you peek, you lose 20 seconds of steam and the temperature drops. Mid-cook replenish: add BOILING water, not cold. Cold water kills the steam instantly. High heat, always. Steaming on medium is a recipe for rubbery buns and translucent fish.

What NOT to Buy

Ultra-thin stainless "steamer inserts": the kind that fold like a flower and fit any pot. They're too shallow, the holes are too big, and food falls through. These are for reheating broccoli, not cooking.

Mini 6-inch bamboo steamers: cute, but you can't fit a fish, a bao, or more than six dumplings. They're decoration.

Electric steamers with timers and "delay start": you're not cooking rice in 1998. These take up counter space and produce weak, inconsistent steam. The stovetop is better.

Plastic steamers: they warp under prolonged heat and the plastic smell infuses into delicate foods like fish and buns.

What to Buy

Amazon links coming soon. In the meantime: look for a 10-12 inch bamboo steamer set (2 tiers + lid), or a heavy-gauge stainless steel steamer pot. Avoid sets that are too lightweight — you want the water reservoir to hold heat. If buying bamboo, make sure the base fits your existing wok or a wide pot. If buying metal, get one with a glass lid so you CAN peek without lifting.
