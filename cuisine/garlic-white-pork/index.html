Suan Ni Bai Rou: A Cold Dish That Lives or Dies by the Cleaver

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-10-29 sichuan cold-dish pork garlic classic

I have been served garlic-white pork maybe sixty times in my life. Fifty of those were wrong, and I can tell you exactly how each one failed before I pick up my chopsticks: the slice was too thick.

Garlic-White Pork (蒜泥白肉, suàn ní bái ròu) is the least famous great dish in Sichuan cooking. No chili heat to hide behind, no numbing tingle, no wok fire. It is boiled pork, cold, with garlic and soy. That's the whole idea. And because there's nothing to hide behind, every mistake shows.

I spent years chasing a good version. In Shanghai the Sichuan places served it as a stack of cold slabs, grey at the edges, with a pool of thin soy and a spoon of chopped garlic dumped on top. Tasted like a salad dressing that had given up. A friend from Chengdu finally told me I was looking in the wrong buildings. "It's not a restaurant dish," she said. "It's a knife dish. Find the man with the knife."

My grandmother made the Shanghai cousin of this dish — plain boiled pork, bái qiē ròu, sliced and dipped in soy with a little scallion oil. No garlic, no sweet soy, no chili oil. She would have called the Sichuan version excessive. She also would have eaten four slices and then asked what was in the sauce.

The version I ended up finding wasn't in a restaurant either. It was a shopfront in Chengdu with no name over the door, four tables, a pot of stock going since morning, and a man at a counter who never once looked up at me.

He lifted a slab of pork out of the pot, still steaming, laid it on a board, put a second board on top, and set a brick on it. Then he waited. Twenty minutes. Nobody in the shop commented on this. The waiting was the recipe.

When he lifted the board the pork had flattened into something with edges like a ruler. He picked up his cleaver — the blade worn thin and curved from years of sharpening — and the slices came off one after another. Thwack. Thwack. Thwack. Each one thin enough that I could read the menu through it. He fanned them onto a plate in a spiral, spooned a dark syrup over the top, and finished with a spoonful of garlic pounded to a wet paste.

I ate it standing up. The slice folded when I lifted it. The fat had gone translucent and firm, not greasy. The garlic hit first, sharp and raw, then the sweet soy came in behind it and smoothed everything out, and at the very end there was a low warm burn from the chili oil that didn't arrive until I'd already swallowed.

The cut matters more than people admit. The classic is 二刀肉 (èrdāo ròu) — the second cut off the hind leg, skin on. It gives you three layers in one slice: skin, a band of fat, then lean with a little grain to it. Pure belly is too soft and collapses when you slice it thin. Loin is a disaster: no fat, no skin, and it dries into shoe leather the moment it hits the air.

Then the pressing. Boil the pork with ginger, scallion and a splash of Shaoxing, then weight it flat while it cools. Unweighted pork is a lump, and a lump cannot be sliced to two millimetres. The weight is why the man with the cleaver could do in one pass what most kitchens can't do at all.

The sauce is where the good places separate from the lazy ones. 复制酱油 (fùzhì jiàngyóu) — "copy soy" — is soy sauce simmered down with brown sugar, star anise, cinnamon, ginger and scallion until it coats the back of a spoon. It's sweet, faintly spiced, and thick enough to cling. Straight soy sauce from the bottle, which is what most places use, tastes like a marinade and sits on the plate instead of on the meat.

And the garlic gets pounded, not chopped. 蒜泥 (suàn ní) literally means garlic mud. Chopped garlic is a pile of sharp little shards that stab you on the way past. Pounded garlic with a pinch of salt turns into a paste that dissolves into the soy and the chili oil and seasons every square millimetre of the slice. It's a five-minute difference that changes the entire dish, and roughly half the kitchens I've eaten in don't bother.

The enemies, then, in order of how often they show up. Thick slices — anything you have to chew has already failed. Fridge-cold pork, which makes the fat coat your mouth like candle wax; this dish wants room temperature. Chopped garlic instead of mud. Bottled soy with no sugar and no body. Lean meat where there should be skin. And the last one, the quiet one: sauce poured over the top like dressing on a salad, so the pork sits dry underneath and only the first slice tastes of anything.

Eat it properly and none of that matters. Pick up one slice, drag it through the garlic settled at the bottom of the pool, fold it once, and eat it in a single bite. Then a mouthful of plain rice — not the pork on the rice, that turns a course into a topping. Then something cold and strong: a cold beer, or a shot of baijiu if the afternoon is already gone.

It costs almost nothing to make. Pork leg, ginger, soy, sugar, garlic, chili oil. The only expensive ingredient is patience, and the only real skill is a knife you keep sharp and a hand that isn't in a hurry.

The man in Chengdu never looked up at me, and I never asked his name. But I watched him press that pork under a brick for twenty minutes, and I understood something about Sichuan food I'd been slow to learn: the famous dishes are loud, and the ones worth driving across a city for are quiet.

Zhouyang grew up in Shanghai and writes about Chinese food, tea, and baijiu for English-speaking readers.
