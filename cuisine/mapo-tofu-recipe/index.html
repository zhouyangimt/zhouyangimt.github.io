How to Cook Real Mapo Tofu: The Sichuan Way

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-06-24 sichuantofuspicyrecipeclassic

Editor’s Note: This page has been rewritten with measured quantities, pan temperatures and the failure points that actually matter. The story behind the dish is at Mapo Tofu: The Pockmarked Granny's Revenge.

Why Mapo Tofu Is a Dish of Two Temperatures

Mapo tofu comes from one stall in Chengdu, opened in 1862 by a widow whose face was marked by smallpox. Chen ma po means Chen the pockmarked grandmother. The original used beef, because beef was what the stall could afford, and soft tofu, because that was what the tofu seller brought round in the morning.

What makes the dish work is that the wok runs at two very different temperatures. Doubanjiang is fermented broad bean paste, and fermentation leaves it with a raw, beany edge that only direct heat drives off. That needs the metal at about 200°C. At that heat the paste darkens from brick red to a deep lacquer and the oil around it turns scarlet, in 45 to 60 seconds. It cannot happen with tofu in the pan, because tofu at 200°C scorches outside and stays cold within.

So the beef and the paste go first, alone. Then the stock goes in and the heat drops to 90°C before the tofu is added, and stays there. Why this matters: at a rolling boil the bubbles collapse against the cubes and knock the corners off, and the churn pushes fat out of the sauce so it separates into oil on top and thin liquid below. At 90°C the surface only shivers, small bubbles at the rim, and the tofu heats through undisturbed.

Starch gelatinises between 62°C and 80°C, so the sauce is already hot enough to set it on contact. A full tablespoon at once sets the outside of each thread before it can disperse, leaving translucent lumps in a sauce that stays thin around them. Three rounds, 20 seconds apart, let each layer coat the tofu before the next builds on it: a thin gloss rather than a jelly at the bottom of the bowl.

Aim for 2:1, tofu to sauce by volume, which here means 400g of cubed tofu against roughly 200ml of finished sauce. If the wok looks like tofu swimming, there is too much liquid. And the tofu is moved, not stirred. The back of a ladle rolls the cubes over each other; the edge of a spoon is a blade at this scale.

Ingredients

For the tofu

  • 400g silken or soft tofu, cut into 2cm cubes
  • 1.2 litres water, held at 80°C
  • 1 tsp (5g) fine salt, for the holding water

For the beef and aromatics

  • 100g minced beef, about 20% fat, chopped coarse rather than to a paste
  • 2 tbsp (30g) Pixian doubanjiang, chopped fine
  • 1 tbsp (18g) douban chilli paste, or 1 tsp chilli flakes
  • 1 tsp (5g) fermented black beans (douchi), rinsed and chopped
  • 1 tbsp (15g) garlic, minced
  • 1 tbsp (12g) fresh ginger, minced
  • 2 spring onions (30g), greens sliced thin on the diagonal

For the sauce and finish

  • 250ml unsalted beef or chicken stock, or water
  • 3 tbsp (45ml) neutral oil
  • 1 tsp (3g) potato starch or cornflour
  • 2 tbsp (30ml) cold water, for the slurry
  • 1/4 tsp (1g) caster sugar
  • 1 tsp (2g) Sichuan pepper, ground fresh
  • 1 tsp (5ml) sesame oil, optional

Substitution notes: Silken tofu gives the softest, most fragile result and is what the Chengdu original uses; soft tofu is a little more forgiving and is what I keep in the fridge. Firm tofu will work but stays chalky in the middle, so blanch it in the 80°C water for 2 minutes first. If you cannot find douban chilli paste, use 1 tsp of dried chilli flakes with 1/2 tsp of sugar; the paste adds colour and a fruity heat, the flakes heat without the fruit. Beef is the original meat and the coarse, dry crumb it gives is part of the texture; pork is the common modern swap, but it makes a softer, sweeter dish.

Prep: Fifteen Minutes, Mostly Careful Cutting

1. Cut and hold the tofu (10 minutes)

Turn the block out onto a board and cut it into 2cm cubes with the knife pressed straight down rather than sawed, because a sawing motion drags and tears the surface. Work in three passes: halves, quarters, cubes. Slide them into a wide pan of 1.2 litres of water with 1 tsp of salt, heated to 80°C. The water is steaming but the bottom of the pan is still quiet, and small bubbles sit on the tofu without breaking the surface. Do not let it boil. Why this matters: at 100°C the protein network contracts hard, squeezes out its own water and leaves the cube porous, so it drinks sauce like a sponge and turns leathery at the edges. The salt firms the surface and seasons the tofu through. Hold it off the heat for up to 20 minutes.

2. Chop the paste and the beans (3 minutes)

Chop the doubanjiang until no lump is bigger than a grain of rice. A lump the size of a pea burns outside and stays raw in the middle, and you will taste it. Rinse the douchi in a sieve for 10 seconds to wash off surface salt, then chop to coarse crumbs. Mince the garlic and ginger separately and finely.

3. Mix the slurry and grind the pepper (2 minutes)

Stir 1 tsp of potato starch into 2 tbsp of cold water; it settles within a minute, so stir again before each addition. Grind the Sichuan pepper and keep it in a small bowl. Pre-ground pepper has usually lost most of its numbing tingle by the time the jar is open.

Step by Step: Twelve Minutes at the Wok

Step 1: Brown the beef at 200°C (2 minutes)

Set a flat-bottomed wok over the highest heat for 2 minutes. A drop of water should evaporate in under a second. Add 2 tbsp of the oil, swirl, add the beef and press it flat with the back of the ladle. Leave it 20 seconds so a crust can form, then break it into crumbs and toss for 60 to 90 seconds, until dry, friable and browned at the edges rather than grey. Push it to one side.

Step 2: Fry the doubanjiang (45 to 60 seconds)

Add the last 1 tbsp of oil to the bare side of the wok, then the doubanjiang, chilli paste and douchi. Stir constantly with the ladle held flat, pressing the paste into the metal. It darkens to a deep red and a clear scarlet oil pools at the edge; the smell turns toasted and the raw bean note goes. If it blackens at the edge, the wok was too hot and there is no rescue. When the oil runs red, add the garlic and ginger and stir for 15 seconds only.

Step 3: Add the stock and come down to 90°C (1 minute)

Pour in the 250ml of stock. It will hiss and rise, and stir as it goes, scraping the base. Add the 1/4 tsp of sugar and bring the liquid to 90°C, which looks like a lazy tremble: small bubbles at the rim, no movement in the middle. Taste now and adjust the salt, because the stock is unsalted and the doubanjiang varies by brand.

Step 4: Slide in the tofu and braise (5 minutes)

Drain the tofu and slide the cubes in from the side of the wok, one edge first, rather than dropping them from a height. Push, do not stir: set the back of the ladle against the sauce and push the tofu from the far side towards you, then from the near side away, so the cubes roll over one another. Do this once a minute for 5 minutes. After the first minute the cubes look slightly swollen; by the fifth the sauce has reduced by about a third and sits just below the top of the tofu.

Step 5: Thicken in three rounds (2 minutes)

Stir the slurry, then add a third of it, about 2 tsp, and fold it through with three or four pushes. Count 20 seconds. Little seems to happen, then the sauce turns from matte to glossy and begins to coat the ladle. Add the second third, wait 20 seconds: when you push the tofu, a trail stays behind for a second before closing. Add the last third only if the sauce still looks loose. The target is a sauce that coats each cube and leaves a faint mark on the bowl, not a gel that moves in one piece.

Step 6: Finish off the heat (30 seconds)

Turn the heat off. Add the ground Sichuan pepper, the scallion greens and the sesame oil, and push the tofu twice through the sauce. Why this matters: the numbing compounds in Sichuan pepper are volatile, and 30 seconds in a 90°C wok drives off much of what you ground the pepper for. Let the wok sit for 2 minutes so the starch network settles and the sauce stops sliding.

Key Pitfalls

The sauce is thin and slides off the tofu. Too much stock, or the starch went in at a boil and never dispersed. Return the wok to 90°C and add slurry 1 tsp at a time, 20 seconds between, until it coats the ladle. Never add dry starch to a hot wok; it clumps and will not smooth out.

The tofu is porous and tastes only of chilli. The holding water boiled, or the tofu was frozen and thawed at some point. At 100°C the cubes contract and open up, and no amount of sauce fixes that texture. Hold them at 80°C, where the water steams but does not move, and check with a thermometer.

The doubanjiang tastes raw and beany. The wok was not hot enough, or the paste was pulled off after 20 seconds. It needs 200°C and a full 45 to 60 seconds, until the oil runs red. Fry it alone, before any liquid goes in, and stir the whole time.

The dish tastes bitter and looks dark brown. The paste or the garlic burned. Garlic added at the start of a 200°C fry is black in 20 seconds, which is why it goes in after the paste has taken its heat. If the paste has caught, start again; burnt chilli cannot be diluted.

The beef is a rubbery lump. Lean beef pressed into a ball at low heat steams instead of browning. Use meat with about 20% fat, get the wok to 200°C, press the mince flat for 20 seconds, then break it up. Mince cooked from frozen always gives grey crumbs.

The sauce is gluey, like wallpaper paste. Too much starch, added in one go. One teaspoon of potato starch is enough for 250ml of stock, and it must go in three rounds so each layer sets before the next arrives. If it has already seized, stir in stock a tablespoon at a time off the heat.

There is no numbing tingle at the end. The pepper was pre-ground, stale, or cooked. Buy whole berries, grind them just before serving, and add them with the heat off.

Serving Suggestions

Serve it in shallow bowls over plain rice, with a spoon so the sauce can be ladled over rather than poured off. The rice should be a little drier than usual, because the sauce is salty and the point is that it soaks in. A plain green vegetable on the side, blanched bok choy or smashed cucumber with vinegar, keeps the meal from being all chilli and oil. The scallion greens go on in the bowl at the last second, so they stay sharp.

To drink, cold beer or plain jasmine tea; the tannins in red wine fight with fermented bean paste. Leftovers keep two days in the fridge, but the tofu weeps and the sauce thickens to a paste, so reheat gently in a pan with 2 tbsp of water rather than in a microwave, which makes the cubes rubbery.
