Youtiao and Soy Milk: The Four Kings of a Shanghai Morning

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-11-08 shanghaibreakfastfriedsoystreet-foodclassic

Six ten in the morning, and the oil is already at temperature. You hear it before you turn the corner — a low hiss that climbs into a roar when the dough goes in, then settles to a crackle like rain on a tin roof. Somewhere behind that sound a woman is counting under her breath, because she has four hundred of these to fry before nine.

The stall is a folding table, a gas burner, a blackened wok of oil, and a plastic tub of dough under a damp towel. No sign. No menu. There is a queue, though, and the queue is the menu: nine people, half of them still in slippers, one man holding a steel pot by the handle because he is buying for the whole family and refuses to let it go cold.

This is 豆浆油条 (dòujiāng yóutiáo), soy milk and fried dough sticks, and it is less a dish than an hour of the day.

The sound before the taste

Two strips of dough, pressed together down the middle with the back of a chopstick, stretched to the length of your forearm, lowered in. Nothing happens for two seconds. Then the seam splits, the stick inflates, and it turns in the oil like something trying to swim. Ninety seconds, maybe less, and it comes out the colour of a copper pot, dripping, and gets stood upright in a wire rack to drain — the whole rack leaning like a bookshelf in an earthquake.

You tear one open and steam comes out of the hollow. That is the entire point of the thing: a crust that shatters, an inside that is nothing but air and a few webs of tender dough, and a seam that stays slightly thicker so you get one soft bite in the middle of all that crunch.

Bad youtiao is dense. It sits in your hand like a battery. Somebody used too much ammonia or alum to make it puff, or the oil was too cool, and now it is a greasy sponge that tastes of nothing but the fryer. You can hear the difference when you break it: a dull snap instead of a shatter. Put it down.

What the bag is for

Shanghai calls this set the four kings — 四大金刚, sì dà jīn gāng: the flat sesame cake, the youtiao, the soy milk, the sticky rice roll. My father’s version of Saturday morning was to hand me a thermos and two yuan and send me down six flights of stairs. I was eight. The stall was two blocks away and I was allowed to walk it alone, which tells you what Shanghai was like in 1999.

The soy milk comes out of a cauldron, not a carton. Ground that morning, boiled hard, and it arrives with a skin on top that you lift out with your chopsticks if you are patient and eat separately if you are not. Sweet, in Shanghai: a spoon of sugar stirred in until it dissolves, and the last mouthful is grit and sweetness and you tilt the bowl for it.

Beijing will fight you on this. Theirs is savoury — vinegar, chilli oil, dried shrimp, pickled vegetable, a few torn pieces of youtiao floating in it like croutons. Both are correct. Order whichever one your grandmother ordered.

The history is short. Youtiao began as political graffiti: Song dynasty, a pair of dough figures fried in effigy of the traitor Qin Hui and his wife, sold as 油炸桧 — “fried Hui.” Eight hundred years on, the figures became two strips, the politics wore off, and the breakfast stayed. That is the whole story and it is enough.

The dunk has a clock on it

Here is where people go wrong. You do not bite a youtiao first. You break it in half, hold it by the top, and push the torn end into the soy milk for about three seconds. Longer and it collapses into the bowl. Shorter and you have made it wet for no reason at all.

What you want is the gradient: soggy at the tip, softening through the middle, still crisp where your fingers are. One stick, four dunks, and what is left at the bottom of the bowl is thicker than soy milk and full of oil and crumbs.

You eat standing up. That is not a rule, it is just what happens — the table is crowded, the stool is taken, you have got a bag in one hand. Nobody sits. Nobody says much either. Six in the morning is a quiet hour in a loud city, and everybody is busy.

The enemies, since we are being honest. The “Chinese doughnut” in a Western bakery case, rolled in sugar and cinnamon: wrong on both counts, because youtiao is not sweet and it is not a doughnut, it is a delivery system for soy milk. The carton: shelf-stable soy milk with vanilla and nine grams of sugar, cold from the fridge — fine as a drink, useless for this, because the job needs heat and a skin and no sweetness beyond what you put in yourself. And the four-in-the-afternoon youtiao, reheated under a lamp, soft since eleven. Frying is not a state, it is an event. This is a food with a fifteen-minute window and no apology.

Which is why the whole thing only exists at dawn. By nine the oil is tired, the rack is empty, and the woman with the chopsticks has wiped the table down and gone home. If you want this breakfast, you get up for it. That is not a hardship. That is the price of the only meal in China that has never once pretended to be brunch.

Zhouyang grew up in Shanghai and writes about Chinese food, tea, and baijiu for English-speaking readers.
