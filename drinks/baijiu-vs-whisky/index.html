One Barrel, One Jar: Baijiu, Whisky, and Two Ways to Wait

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-11-14 baijiuwhiskyscotlandcomparisonfermentation

In a dunnage warehouse outside Elgin, the cold reaches your shoes first. Damp stone floor, stone walls, and on both sides of a narrow aisle the oak casks lie three high, their heads chalked with numbers in a hand copied from cask to cask for decades. The air smells of wet wood, vanilla, a little sulfur, and bread from somewhere two doors down.

The warehouseman draws a bung from a 2011 hogshead and hands me a glass at cask strength. It is twenty past nine in the morning. Nobody in the room finds this odd.

Six weeks earlier I stood in a different cellar, outside Luzhou in Sichuan. Unglazed clay jars the height of a man, sealed under mud and cloth, no oak in sight, the air warm and sour with sorghum. Same plan in both places: take grain, make a clear spirit, then wait. Almost nothing else matches. Whisky and baijiu are one craft run on two opposite rulebooks, and once you find where the rulebooks split, both drinks get easier to taste.

Where the grain turns to sugar

Whisky starts by malting. The barley is wetted and allowed to sprout, which switches on the grain's own enzymes, then it is kilned dry. Those enzymes do their work in the mash tun, cutting starch into sugar, and a few days later yeast turns that sugar into alcohol in a washback. The order is fixed: sugar first, alcohol second. Thirteen years of writing build pipelines taught me to distrust that kind of tidiness.

Baijiu never sprouts anything. It uses qu, a brick of grain colonised by mold and yeast, and throws starch conversion and fermentation into the same vessel at the same time. Steamed sorghum is mixed with crushed qu, packed into pits dug in the ground or into jars, and left for a month or two with no water added. The mass stays solid the whole way through. Whisky is a two-stage build; baijiu compiles and links in one pass. Song Yingxing wrote the rule down in 1637, in Tiangong Kaiwu: 凡酿酒必资曲药成信 — whatever grain you use, you cannot make wine without qu to carry the fermentation. Four centuries later that sentence still describes, literally, how every bottle of baijiu is made. I went deeper on that split in the qu piece.

Oak versus clay

This is where the two spirits really part. Scotch has a legal minimum of three years in oak, in Scotland, and the cask is not storage. It is an ingredient. American oak gives vanilla and coconut lactones, European oak gives tannin and dried fruit, a first-fill sherry butt adds raisins on top of both, and the wood strips out sulfur compounds left over from distillation. A whisky is roughly half distillery and half cask, and every distiller will tell you so.

Baijiu matures in unglazed clay, and clay gives nothing back. No vanillin, no tannin, no colour — a ten-year-old baijiu is exactly as clear as a one-year-old. What the jar does is breathe. The walls are porous enough for slow micro-oxygenation and for the harshest volatiles to leave; in a cellar in Sichuan you can smell the loss, a sweet-sour breath hanging over the rows. The Chinese word for it is 挥发, volatilisation, and cellars count on it the way a Scottish warehouse counts its angels' share.

Oak is additive. Clay is subtractive. A blender in Scotland builds flavour inside the barrel; a blender in China has to have built it before the jar, and can only wait for it to calm down.

Why "blended" is not a dirty word

Both trades blend, and both get insulted for it. In whisky, blended is an ordinary category; a single malt is itself a blend of dozens of casks. Baijiu calls the same work 勾调, gou tiao, and it is the most senior job in the plant. A 52% bottle of strong-aroma baijiu is assembled from base spirits of several pit ages and several grades, adjusted in tiny increments until the house profile lands. The word you will meet instead is 勾兑, gou dui, which got translated as "adulteration" during a scandal in the 1990s and never recovered its name. It means blending. If anything the Chinese version is the harder discipline, because there is no barrel to hide behind.

Two industries, two sets of numbers

China produced 13.58 million kilolitres of baijiu in 2016. By 2024 the figure was about 4.1 million — down by two thirds in eight years, while revenue rose. The cheap end of the market is gone. Scotland exported 5.4 billion pounds of whisky in 2024, roughly 1.4 billion bottles, and sends most of what it makes across a border. Baijiu exports somewhere near one percent of its output. Two grain spirits of similar strength, and one is a global product while the other is a domestic habit. That gap is not about quality. It is about who the drink is for.

A private drink and a public one

Whisky is built to be met alone: one glass, one chair, twenty minutes, and a bottle that lasts months. Nobody keeps score.

Baijiu is built for a table. Small cups, all filled at the same moment, a toast order that runs outward from the host, and a bottle that will be empty by ten. The etiquette has one sentence that explains it all: 我干了，你随意 — I finish mine, you drink as you please. It sounds like courtesy; it is a public accounting of how much each person has committed. The drink belongs to the group. That is also why baijiu sits at 52 or 53 percent: it is built to be eaten with, cutting through cold pork and braised fat, not nursed for an hour.

If you come from whisky

Three rules. Break any of them and a good bottle will taste like solvent.

Room temperature, no ice. At 52%, ice does not dilute so much as collapse the aroma: the long-chain esters that carry the smell drop out of solution, and the drink goes flat and faintly oily. If a bottle feels too hot, three drops of room-temperature water will open it. A cube will shut it.

Smell from the lip of the cup, mouth open. Nose a baijiu the way you nose a 43% single malt and you numb yourself in two seconds, and numbed you taste nothing. Short sniffs, low, from the rim.

Eat something. This is not a spirit for an empty stomach or a quiet room. Order the cold dish.

For a first bottle, read the back label: 固态法白酒, solid-state fermented, and a standard number starting GB/T 10781. If it says 液态法 or lists 食用酒精 among the ingredients, it is grain alcohol with aroma added and none of the above applies. Start around a hundred to two hundred renminbi with a Fenjiu or a Luzhou Tequ, not a gift box at ten times the price. Cheap to decent is an enormous jump. Decent to gift box is mostly cardboard.
