Chinese Alcohol 101: Where Baijiu, Huangjiu and Rice Wine Split Apart

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-10-10 baijiuhuangjiumijiufermentationbeginner-guide

On the first night of Lunar New Year, my aunt's table in Shanghai carries three drinks and one word.

At one end stands a bottle of clear liquid at 52 percent, poured into glasses the size of a thimble. In the middle, a clay jug of amber wine sits in a bowl of hot water, steam curling off the top. At the end nearest the kitchen is a jar of my grandmother's 酒酿 (jiu niang) — sweet fermented rice, faintly fizzy, eaten with a spoon.

In English those are a spirit, a wine, and a dessert. In Chinese they are all 酒 (jiu). The word has not changed in three thousand years, and it covers everything from a warm bowl of soft rice to a bottle that sells for the price of a car. They are not variations on one drink. They are four different drinks sharing one name, and until you know where they part, every Chinese liquor shelf will look like noise. This is the map.

It All Starts With Qu

Every one of them begins the same way: grain, water, and 曲 (qu).

Qu is a brick of grain — wheat, barley, or rice — moistened, pressed, and left in a warm room until mold grows all the way through it. The mold is the point. It releases enzymes that cut starch into sugar, and the same brick carries the yeast that turns that sugar into alcohol. One object, two jobs.

The West splits those jobs. It sprouts barley into malt, which uses its own enzymes to make sugar, then adds yeast to ferment that sugar into drink. China packs both into a single starter and lets molds, yeasts and bacteria sort out their territory inside the brick before brewing even starts.

That one decision is the root of everything below. The method is less a recipe than a managed riot of microbes, and it throws off flavors a winemaker would call faults: soy sauce, overripe pineapple, damp cellar, a whiff of petrol. Chinese drinkers call them the point.

The Fork in the Road

After fermentation, the families split on a single question: do you distill it, or not?

Fermented. Stop at the fermentation and you have the oldest Chinese drinks. 黄酒 (huang jiu), literally "yellow wine," is fermented glutinous rice — amber, 15 to 20 percent, and old enough to have been drunk before the first emperor. It tastes of caramel and dried dates with a soft savory edge, closer to a dry sherry than to anything made from a grape. Its everyday cousin is 米酒 (mi jiu), rice wine, and at its sweetest end comes 酒酿 (jiu niang), the fermented rice sold in every market, barely alcoholic and often eaten for breakfast.

Distilled. Run that fermented mash through a still and you get 白酒 (bai jiu): 40 to 60 percent, clear, and sharp enough to strip paint. This is the young one. Distillation reached China later than it reached Europe, and baijiu only became the country's everyday drink in the last century. The technique changed; the starter did not. Within baijiu sit a dozen aroma types, led by four, but that argument belongs to another piece.

Fruit wines (果酒) and medicinal soaks (药酒) round out the shelf, but they are footnotes to the two big families, and they follow the same logic: ferment the sugar, then decide whether to distill.

The fastest way to tell which world you are in is the number on the label. Below about 20 percent, you are drinking something fermented — a huangjiu, a rice wine, a fruit wine. Above 35, you are drinking something distilled.

One Character, Nine Thousand Years

The character 酒 is older than writing itself. When archaeologists opened a pottery jar at Jiahu in Henan, they found the residue of a fermented drink made from rice, honey and wild fruit — around nine thousand years old, the earliest alcoholic beverage anyone has identified anywhere. The grain-based, mold-driven logic was there at the very start.

The reason all of it still lives under one word is social, not chemical. In the West, wine belongs to the dinner table and spirits belong to the bar; the two crowds rarely meet. In China the same liquid, at any strength, runs through the same channels — the banquet, the toast, the offering to ancestors, the deal closed over dinner. There is no wine culture here and a separate spirits culture. There is one culture, and its word is 酒.

How to Read a Chinese Liquor Shelf

Three rules, in order.

Read the number first. Under 20 percent is fermented; over 35 is distilled. That single figure tells you whether you are about to sip or brace.

Read the ingredient line second. If it says 固态发酵, solid-state fermentation, the flavor came from microbes. If it says 液态法 or lists 食用酒精 (edible alcohol), you are holding grain alcohol with flavoring added — fine in a braise, a waste of a glass.

Then match the bottle to the occasion. Amber and meant to be warmed: huangjiu, served at 40 to 45°C in a small cup, a winter drink that turns a plain meal into a slow one. Clear and burning: baijiu, sipped from a thimble, never shot. Cloudy, sweet and spoonable: jiuniang, breakfast or dessert, barely a drink at all.

If you have never had Chinese alcohol, do not start with baijiu. Start with a bottle of ten-year Shaoxing huangjiu — I keep one in my Shanghai kitchen for cold nights and for braising pork alike. Warm it, pour a small glass, and let it be gentle with you. The clear 52-percent stuff is not going anywhere; it will still be there when you have earned it.
