The Secret in the Qu: Dongjiu and the 130 Herbs Nobody Will Name

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-11-15 baijiuguizhoumedicinal-aromafermentation

Six forty in the morning in Donggongsi, and the fog has not lifted off the rail line. I eat a bowl of mutton rice noodles standing up, the way everyone else on the street does, then walk uphill past a grey wall with a gate in it. Zunyi smells of wet asphalt and chili oil. Behind that wall it smells like a pharmacy.

The qu room is warm enough to fog your glasses. Wooden shelves run to the ceiling, and the qu bricks sit on them in tiers, each one a little smaller than a shoebox, dusted white at the corners. A woman in a blue apron turns one over so I can see the underside: furred grey-green with mold, and through the fur, pale flecks of crushed stem and root. Cardamom. Dried citrus peel. Something bitter and woody I cannot name. The room has been making this exact smell since before anyone working in it was born.

Every baijiu in China is fermented by qu, a cake of grain that mold and yeast have colonised. Dongjiu is the one that folds herbs into the cake, more than 130 of them, and the one whose formula is a state secret. Everything else about the bottle follows from those two facts.

What the word "aroma type" is doing in the sentence

China sorts baijiu into aroma types the way France sorts cheese by valley: 酱香 sauce, 浓香 strong, 清香 light, 米香 rice, and eight more that most drinkers never meet. The national terminology counts twelve. Dongjiu is 董香型, the Dong aroma type, and nearly everyone outside the trade also calls it 药香型, medicinal aroma. It is the only entry on the list defined by a plant list.

The mash that never becomes alcohol

Dongjiu runs two fermentations side by side, and only one of them is trying to make alcohol.

The first uses 小曲, a small rice-based qu that is also herbed, in small pits. It ferments the 酒醅, the mash that will actually be distilled. The second uses 大曲, a wheat qu carrying the full herb bill, in big pits, and ferments what the plant calls 香醅: a mash built entirely for smell. It ferments for months and it is never distilled for its own alcohol, because there is hardly any to take.

Then comes the step that makes the style. The two mashes go into the same still, and the alcohol vapour rising off the 酒醅 is forced through the layer of 香醅 on its way out. If you have ever watched a query pass through an index, you already know the shape of it. The alcohol is the query. The 香醅 is the index. What comes out the other side has been rewritten by everything it touched.

This is worth stating plainly, because most people assume the opposite: no herbs go into the bottle. Nothing is soaked, infused or blended in afterwards. The herbs are food for microbes, and the microbes are what you taste.

The list

The herbs are ground and mixed into the wheat before the qu is moistened and stacked. Which 130 has been a classified document since 1983, when the formula and the process were designated a state secret, with no expiry date on the designation. In a country that classifies weapons designs and geological surveys, a bottle of liquor sitting on the same list is a strange thing to hold in your hand.

The company publishes the count. It sometimes publishes a partial list of the pharmacopoeia it draws from. It has never published the recipe. So when you read that Dongjiu contains licorice, or dried tangerine peel, or angelica, you are reading a plausible guess dressed up as a fact. What is not a guess is the smell.

Herbs in qu are not a Guizhou invention. In the sixth century, Jia Sixie's farming manual Qimin Yaoshu already has the brewer folding 苍耳, 艾 and 野蓼 into his starter, cocklebur and mugwort and smartweed, and the reason was practical as much as aromatic: the herbs helped hold back the wrong microbes while the right ones grew. Fourteen hundred years later, the same logic, a longer list, and a bigger still.

The house tasting note, printed in the distillery's own material, reads 清澈透明，香气幽雅舒适，入口醇和浓郁，饮后甘爽味长: clear, a restrained aroma, soft and full in the mouth, clean and long afterwards. My own note is less polite. The first second is sweet and round, closer to a strong-aroma baijiu than to anything medicinal. Then the back of the mouth fills with roots, licorice and dried peel and a bitter green edge, and the finish is cool and dry, like the air in that qu room. It does not taste like medicine because someone added medicine. It tastes like medicine because the entire fermentation was built around it.

The town with two famous drinks

In 1963 the second national wine assessment put eight baijiu on its top list, and two came from Zunyi prefecture: Moutai, up in the mountains at Renhuai, and Dongjiu, here in the city. One became the most expensive spirit in the world. The other stayed a local habit, which is either a failure or a very Chinese kind of success.

The 1990s were not kind to it. Costs were cut, the herb bill was thinned, the bottle lost the thing that made it worth arguing about, and by the early 2000s the plant had gone quiet. It came back when the old two-qu process was rebuilt, and the older bottles, the ones filled before the drift, now change hands among collectors for many times what they cost.

How to drink it without flinching

Three rules.

Room temperature, no ice, and keep it out of the fridge. At 54% the aromatics are already at the edge of what a nose can hold. Cold drops the long-chain esters out of solution and the pharmacy collapses into a wet cellar.

Pour it and wait two minutes. That is enough for the sharp top note to burn off and the 酯香 to come forward. Dongjiu is the one baijiu where the first sniff is actively misleading.

Eat something. Braised beef, cured sausage, cold pork with garlic. This is a table drink, built to cut fat, and it is at its best around food that fights back.

Buying is easy once you know the words. Look for 董香型 on the label and 54% on the front. The 国密 bottles are the current flagship. Skip anything marketed as a health tonic: the herbs are there for aroma, nobody has shown the drink does anything for your body, and the sellers who imply otherwise are why this category has a reputation problem. Try 50ml at a bar before you commit to 500ml.

If you hate it, you are in large company. Dongjiu is the most polarising bottle in China, and the people who love it love it the way people love a very smelly cheese: not in spite of the smell.
