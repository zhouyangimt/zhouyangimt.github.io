Huangjiu 101: China's Oldest Alcohol, and Why the West Has Never Heard of It

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-10-11 huangjiushaoxingfermentationbeginner-guidezhejiang

On the coldest nights in Shanghai, my grandmother did not open the good bottle. She opened the cheap one.

She poured a dark amber liquid into a small porcelain jug, dropped in two slivers of ginger and a dried plum, and stood the jug in a bowl of just-boiled water. The smell came up before the drink did: caramel, dried dates, something a little like soy sauce left in the sun. When she finally poured it, the glass fogged and the whole kitchen felt warm.

That was 黄酒 (huang jiu), yellow wine. It is the oldest alcohol China has — older than baijiu by more than two thousand years — and for most of Chinese history it was simply the drink. The one in the poems. The one at the wedding. The one poured onto the ground for the ancestors. The clear firewater the world now calls "Chinese liquor" is the newcomer; huangjiu is the elder.

And almost nobody outside China has tasted a real one. If you have met huangjiu at all, there is a decent chance you met the worst version of it. Here is what it actually is, and why it never left home.

A Wine, Not a Spirit

Huangjiu is fermented, not distilled. That one fact explains most of what follows. Grain goes in, microbes do their work, and what comes out is filtered and drunk — no still, no 52 percent, no thimble glasses. A good Shaoxing bottling lands between 14 and 20 percent alcohol, which puts it in the same weight class as a dry sherry or a rich white Burgundy. It is meant to be sipped, slowly, and it is very easy to forget that it is strong.

The grain is mostly 糯米 (glutinous rice), and the starter is not the big brick of qu that baijiu leans on, but 麦曲 (mai qu), a wheat-based mold culture, plus a pinch of 酒药 (jiu yao), a living yeast-and-mold starter kept and fed like sourdough. Then comes the trick that defines this whole family: the mold cuts starch into sugar at the same moment the yeast turns that sugar into alcohol — two jobs running side by side in one vat. I spent thirteen years at Microsoft, Google and NVIDIA watching processes fight over shared state, and this is roughly that situation. Except here the contention is the recipe, and the flavor comes from how gracefully the two take turns.

That parallel fermentation leaves behind things grape wine never has: amino acids, peptides, and sugars the yeast could not finish. On the tongue they read as caramel, dried jujube, toasted nuts, and a long savory tail that is unmistakably umami — the same register as soy sauce or aged ham. It is why huangjiu is the rare drink that works both as something to sip and as something to season with, without changing its character in between.

To keep the wine from turning, Song-dynasty makers heated it gently before sealing the jar — a step called 煎酒 (jian jiu). The oldest surviving manual that describes it, the Beishan Jiujing, is from 1117. Louis Pasteur published his method for killing microbes with heat in 1864. Chinese winemakers were doing exactly that, on purpose, roughly 750 years earlier, for exactly the same reason. They just never wrote a French paper about it.

Three Thousand Years, and One River

Shaoxing, the small city south of Shanghai that gives this wine its name, has been making it since at least the Spring and Autumn period — around 2,500 years ago, when nobody in Europe was writing anything down about vintages. One local story is worth keeping: when King Goujian of Yue marched his army to war, he poured a single jug of wine into the river and let the whole column drink from the current downstream. The point was not the alcohol. The point was that everyone at the table shares the same cup, even the man holding a spear. That idea — the cup as a bond rather than a buzz — is the spine of Chinese drinking culture, and it was born in huangjiu.

This is also the drink in the poetry. When Li Bai drank alone under the moon, when Su Shi wrote himself half-drunk on a river boat, none of them were drinking baijiu. Distilled spirits barely existed in their world. The "wine" of classical Chinese verse is huangjiu: soft, warm, easy to drink a great deal of, very slowly. It is the kind of drunk that produces an elegy instead of a bar fight.

Why It Never Left

So why does a drink this old, this good, and this central stay invisible in the West? Four reasons, and none of them is about quality.

It is a living wine, and it does not travel. Huangjiu is full of dissolved solids and unspent sugars, and it oxidizes fast once the seal is broken. Port and sherry survived the sea because they were fortified to last for years on a shelf in London; huangjiu was never built for that, because it never had to be.

The English name is a disaster. "Yellow wine" sounds like a country project or a rice vinegar; "rice wine" collides with sake. Japan's rice wine got a name — sake — and a global identity with it. China's got a literal translation nobody can picture.

The kitchen ate it. A large share of the huangjiu made today goes into food, and the 料酒 (cooking wine) on supermarket shelves is low-grade huangjiu, salted so you will not drink it. If you have ever "had huangjiu," there is a real chance you met the working class and never the family above it.

And baijiu beat it at home. Through the twentieth century, cheap grain and high-proof distillation made baijiu the mass drink of a poor, fast-industrializing country. Huangjiu became regional and ceremonial — the thing you warm on a cold night, or pour at a wedding, rather than the thing on every table.

How to Drink It Without Ruining It

Three rules, and they matter more than the label.

Warm it, but do not boil it. Around 40 to 45°C — the temperature of a bath you would trust with a baby. Stand the bottle, or a small jug, in a bowl of hot water for five minutes. A microwave works but cooks the delicate aromatics flat, so skip it. Warmth is not a ritual; it releases the caramel and date notes cold suppresses.

Buy 加饭 (jiafan) or 花雕 (huadiao), not the cheapest "cooking" grade. Both are dry-to-medium Shaoxing styles, widely exported, and a decent 500ml bottle costs less than a mid-range cocktail. Shaoxing huangjiu also carries a protected geographic mark, so look for the city name on the label. At the bottom of the range you are drinking an ingredient; at the top, a wine.

Give it food, and give it autumn. The classic pairing is 大闸蟹, hairy crab, steamed and dipped in vinegar — the wine's savory sweetness is built to cut the crab's rich roe. If you have no crab, a braised pork belly or a plate of salted peanuts will do. And finish the bottle within a week or two of opening, kept cold. Unlike spirits, this one dies.
