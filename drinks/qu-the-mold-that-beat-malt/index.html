The Mold That Beat Malt: How Qu Split Chinese Alcohol From the West

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-11-08 qufermentationbaijiuhuangjiuscience

The qu room in Fenyang runs at forty-five degrees in June and smells like a bakery someone forgot to close. Barley and peas, steamed and crushed, pressed into bricks and stood on edge in rows with a hand's width between them. Every brick wears a coat of white fur. The fur is the point. A foreman turns the rows twice a day, and the room keeps getting hotter as the mold feeds and throws off heat of its own. By the second week you can smell pears and bread through the door.

Nothing in a Chinese bottle makes sense until you understand what is happening in that room. Qu (曲) is grain colonised on purpose by molds, yeasts and bacteria, then dried and ground and pitched into a mash. It is not an ingredient. It is a factory you build once a year, run hot, and then kill at the right moment.

Two Ways to Unlock Starch

Starch is sugar in a locked box. A grain of barley or sorghum is mostly long chains of glucose, and yeast cannot eat chains. Every alcoholic drink on earth is therefore a story about one enzyme: amylase, the key that cuts the chain.

Europe found the key inside the seed. Wet barley in a cool room thinks spring has come, and in sprouting it releases its own amylase. Stop it with heat at the right hour and you have malt, grain carrying the enzyme that unlocks its own starch. Malt gives you a liquid wort you can boil, strain and pitch with one chosen yeast.

China found the same key in a fungus. Instead of sprouting the grain, the Chinese steamed it and let mold move in. The mold grows through the mass, sinking enzymes into starch it cannot swallow, and the sugars it leaves behind feed the yeasts and bacteria living beside it. Same chemistry, different landlord. And because the mold works in a damp solid rather than a liquid, you never strain anything. You ferment in the grain itself and distill the whole mess afterward.

That is the fork. One road ends in beer and whisky, the other in baijiu and huangjiu.

What Is Actually in the Brick

The recipe changes every few hundred kilometres. Fenyang presses barley with peas, and the peas give the beany sweetness behind light-aroma baijiu. Maotai uses wheat alone. Shaoxing presses wheat cakes for huangjiu, and in the south they roll rice and herbs into balls the size of a plum.

A brick the size of a fat book feels lighter than it looks, and rougher, like a dried sponge with straw in it. The surface runs through three colours as fermentation goes on: white while the mold is young, then yellow, then a dark brown-black as the colony matures. Workers read those colours the way a baker reads a crust.

Temperature is the dial that decides what kind of liquor you get. Light-aroma qu peaks around 45°C, warm enough to grow mold without cooking it, and pushes flavour toward clarity. Medium-temperature qu climbs into the fifties and picks up more bacteria, which is where the pineapple-and-banana ester comes from. Sauce-aroma qu is deliberately abused, allowed past 60°C until most of the yeasts are dead and only the tough thermophilic species survive.

Which raises the obvious question: if heat kills your yeast, where does the alcohol come from? Sauce-aroma distilleries pile the cooked sorghum on the workshop floor for days before it goes near a pit, turning it by hand so airborne yeast and the surviving spores multiply back in. The Chinese call that open-air pile the sun fermentation. It is a repair step, invented because the qu was made too hot to do the job alone.

The dose is brutal by brewing standards. A strong-aroma distillery mixes in roughly a fifth of the grain weight as qu. Western brewers pitch a fraction of a percent of yeast. Here the starter is also raw material, and every brick brings its own enzymes and its own flavour baggage.

The Road Not Taken

China knew about malt. In the Book of Documents, the 尚书, a minister instructs the king with a line that has been quoted for two and a half thousand years: 若作酒醴，尔惟曲蘖. If you would make wine and sweet wine, you must rely on qu and nie. Qu is the mold starter. Nie (蘖) is sprouted grain. Both technologies named in one sentence around the fourth century BC, both understood.

Then China dropped one. Nie produced 醴, a sweet, low-alcohol grain drink that appears in ritual texts and mostly fades from ordinary life after the Han. Qu won, and by the Ming the outcome was settled enough to be stated as law. Song Yingxing, in 1637, opened the chapter on starters in The Exploitation of the Works of Nature with a flat sentence: 凡酿酒必资曲药成信。无曲即佳米珍黍，空造不成. In all winemaking you must rely on qu as the agent of change; without qu, even fine rice and precious millet will ferment nothing at all.

Why the mold and not the malt? Climate did most of the work. Malting wants cool, dark rooms and a steady hand on the germination bed, which is a northern European problem solved in a northern European way. Southern China is a warm, wet incubator where mold colonises grain whether you invite it or not. Better to enlist it.

Chemistry did the rest. A mold starter brings more than amylase: proteases that cut grain protein into savoury amino acids, lipases that free fatty acids for later esterification, and its own metabolic by-products. That is why Chinese liquor reaches flavours malt whisky only gets from a barrel. The Chinese have a phrase for it, 曲为酒之骨, qu is the bone of the liquor. Grain is the flesh; bone sets the shape.

The twentieth century tried to industrialise the bone. In the 1950s the microbiologist Fang Xinfang and the Yantai pilot plant replaced the brick with steamed bran inoculated with one cultured mold. Bran qu (麸曲) cut the starter from a fifth of the grain bill to a tenth and fermentation from weeks to days. It worked, and it flattened the flavour, which is why most bottles that gave the category its bad name abroad are bran-qu spirits.

How to Taste the Difference

Labels in China are unusually honest if you know two characters. 大曲 (daqu, big qu) means whole grain, slow fermentation. 麸曲 (fuqu, bran qu) means cultured mold on bran, faster and thinner. A bottle that says neither is probably 液态法, liquid fermentation: neutral alcohol plus flavouring.

Standard numbers help more than marketing copy. GB/T 10781.1 is strong aroma, 10781.2 light aroma, 10781.3 rice aroma, GB/T 26760 sauce aroma. If the back label cites 20821 or 20822, you are holding the liquid or blended method.

You can also test it on your own hand. Put a drop of each on a palm, rub until the alcohol flashes off, then smell. A daqu spirit leaves bread crust, bran and a bitter almond edge on the skin. A liquid-fermented one leaves almost nothing, or a sweet, slightly soapy ghost. It takes thirty seconds and it has never lied to me.

The cheapest way to meet the mold is not a bottle at all. Buy a packet of 甜酒曲, the sweet rice starter sold in any Chinese grocery, steam glutinous rice, cool it, mix, and leave it in a bowl for two days. You come back to rice swimming in its own sweet liquid, faintly fizzy, smelling of flowers. That is qu doing what it did in Fenyang, minus the heat.

Next: baijiu and whisky are both grain spirits, both distilled, both aged, and they taste nothing alike. The reason is not the still but the starter, the barrel, and the fact that one tradition drinks its history while the other drinks its weather.
