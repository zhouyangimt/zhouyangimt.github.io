The Rules of the Table: Why Your Glass Has to Sit Lower

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

2026-10-31 baijiuritualetiquettebanquetshanghai

It was a seafood restaurant off Hongqiao Road, the kind with no signboard and a private room on the second floor. Eleven of us at a table set for fourteen. A plate of cold braised beef went round and round on the lazy susan because nobody had picked up their chopsticks. In front of the host stood ten small glasses in a row, each one filled to the rim.

He stood up. Eleven people stood up. That took four seconds, and it was the first thing the table taught me: this room has a shape, and you can read it from where the glasses are.

What followed was not drinking. It was a protocol, executed correctly, for two hours. 主陪 the host, 副陪 the co-host, three cups taken together, then a round of individual toasts in an order nobody had written down and everybody knew. By ten o'clock two men had left the room to be sick, one had made a speech about 2008, and the host had barely drunk at all.

That last detail is the one worth keeping. The man who poured the most drank the least. 酒桌, jiǔzhuō, the drinking table, is not a party. It is a ranking system that happens to have food on it.

The Table Has a Shape

Start with the seats. In the north, Shandong especially, seating is not casual. The host sits facing the door. The guest of honour sits on his right. The co-host sits opposite the host with his back to the door, and works the room from below like a vice president. Everyone else fills in by rank, and if you are a foreigner you get put somewhere safe, usually near the co-host, whose job for the evening is to keep you upright.

Then the order of the toasts. The host opens with three cups taken together, 集体三杯. Nobody eats before this. Only afterwards does the table break into single toasts, 打圈, one person at a time: you stand, you say something short, you drink, they drink, both sit. If someone toasts you, you owe a return toast in the same round. Not later. Later is a debt with interest.

The accounting is the whole point. Every toast is one unit of respect, and the table keeps a running total in public. A toast you accept and never return sits there unacknowledged, and the table notices the way a room notices a phone that keeps ringing. On a table of twelve, one full round costs you eleven cups.

Which is why the glass is small and why the arithmetic matters. A 500ml bottle of 52 percent baijiu holds about 205 grams of pure alcohol, roughly fifteen American standard drinks. Six bottles spread across twelve people is still about seven drinks each before the beer arrives. The small glass does not make the evening gentle. It makes the evening countable.

Why Your Glass Has to Sit Lower

The rule a foreigner notices first is the rim. When you toast someone senior, you hold the glass in both hands, left palm flat under the base, and you bring your rim below the rim of theirs. Do it at a Chinese table and you trigger a small polite fight, two people pushing their glasses downward at each other, each trying to be the lower one. The gesture says: I place myself under you, in a unit everybody at the table can see.

Then there is the filling. Wine goes to the brim. Tea never does. 酒满敬人，茶满欺人, a full glass of wine honours the guest, a full cup of tea insults him, because a brimming teacup is too hot to hold and quietly tells you to leave. The three-qian cup, 三钱杯, holds about ten millilitres, so a full glass is a swallow rather than a session.

Watch the verb, too. 干杯, gānbēi, means dry the cup, and it is meant literally. 随意, suíyì, means as you like, and it is the release valve. Hosts use both in the same evening, and the whole game is hearing which one you were given. Answering 随意 with a 干杯 is fine. Answering 干杯 with a sip is a statement.

And you do not pour your own. Your neighbours fill your glass; you fill theirs, starting with the person on your right. When someone tops you up, tap two fingers on the table, 叩指礼. The story is that a Qing emperor travelling in disguise poured tea for his ministers, who could not kneel in public and tapped their fingers instead of their foreheads. Whether or not that happened, the gesture is now the cheapest thank-you in China and everyone uses it.

Three Thousand Years of the Same Advice

The oldest Chinese drinking manual is a poem, and it spends its second half describing a guest who ruins the evening. In 《诗经·小雅·宾之初筵》, around 800 BC, the guests arrive 温温其恭, gentle and respectful; a few cups later their caps are askew and they are dancing badly. Then the line that survived: 既醉而出，并受其福；醉而不出，是谓伐德. Leave when you are drunk and you take the blessing with you; stay when you are drunk and you are cutting down your own character. Twenty-eight centuries of etiquette, and the core instruction is know when to go home.

The politics arrived early too. 《尚书·酒诰》, the Duke of Zhou's edict after the conquest of Shang, is the first anti-binge law on record, and its target is not the individual drunk but the group: 群饮, collective drinking, seize them all and send them to Zhou, and I will put them to death. The Zhou had just watched the Shang aristocracy drink itself into a conquered dynasty. Group drinking read as group conspiracy.

Even the great gourmands pushed back. Yuan Mei, in his eighteenth-century cookbook 《随园食单》, wrote 事之是非，惟醒人能知之；味之美恶，亦惟醒人能知之. Only a sober man can judge whether a thing is right, and only a sober man can judge whether a dish is good. He kept wine. He also kept his tongue.

How to Get Through It

Eight rules, with the reason attached.

Stand when the host stands. Sit only after he sits. This is the entire hierarchy in one motion, and you get it for free.

Two hands, rim low, left palm under the base, when you toast anyone older or more senior. Do not fight the downward push for more than one round. Let them win.

Never pour your own glass. Fill your neighbours' first, right side before left, and tap the table when yours is filled.

Listen for 干杯 or 随意 before you commit. If you did not hear it, take a small sip and watch what the others do.

Return every toast in the same round, even with a sip if you have already declared yourself out.

Eat. Constantly. The food is not decoration, it is the buffer that separates six bottles from a good evening and six bottles from a bad one.

Declare your exit in the first ten minutes, not the third hour. 我开车, I am driving; 医生不让, my doctor said no; or 以茶代酒, tea in place of wine, which is accepted if you say it early and never renegotiate at the fourth toast. A limit announced up front is a position. A limit discovered at midnight is an insult.

Do not flip the fish. 翻鱼 sounds like 翻船, capsizing the boat, and on any table with a fish the head is loaded anyway: in Henan and Shandong, 头三尾四, three cups for whoever the head points at, four for the tail. Lift the spine out instead, and keep your glass where it is.
