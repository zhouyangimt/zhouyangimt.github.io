The Fairness Cup: Why You Never Pour the First Cup Straight From the Pot

The first time I poured tea for a table of six, I did it wrong, and nobody said a word. It was in Shantou, in a back room of a shop that sold dried shrimp and dancong by the armful. There was a gaiwan, six cups the size of walnut shells, and a small glass jug I ignored because I did not know what it was for. I lifted the lid and poured straight into the cups, one after another, starting at my left. The first cup came out almost black. By the sixth it was the color of weak honey and tasted like the memory of tea. An old man across the table picked up that last cup, looked into it, put it down, and reached past me for the glass jug. "先倒进去," he said. Pour it in here first.

That jug is the fairness cup, 公道杯, gōngdào bēi. It is the least glamorous object on a Chinese tea table, and the one that quietly fixes the thing that ruins most home-brewed tea: your first cup and your last cup are not the same drink.

Gongfu brewing means many short infusions from the same leaf. A 120ml gaiwan might give eight or ten pours, twenty seconds each, and every one tastes different. The first is sharp and perfumed, the fourth is round and sweet, the eighth is a shadow of both. That is not a flaw. That is the entire point of drinking this way.

There is a second, subtler variation inside every single pour. Water hits the leaf and starts dissolving it the instant they touch. The liquid that leaves the gaiwan in the first second is more concentrated than the liquid that leaves three seconds later, and the last of it, pooled down near the leaf, is thinner still. So if you pour straight into four cups, you have not served one tea four times. You have served four teas. The first person gets the extract. The last gets the rinse.

The fairness cup is a buffer. You pour the whole infusion into it at once, the strong beginning and the pale end mix into one liquid, and then you serve from that. Every cup is identical. In a culture where the host pours for everyone and the eldest drinks first, that is not a small thing. 公道 is justice, the public way — the same measure for everyone at the table, enforced by a piece of glass that costs less than a sandwich.

I spent thirteen years at Microsoft, Google and NVIDIA, and the bug is one I know from servers. A batch job that hands off results as they come out of the queue gives the first worker a full payload and the last an empty one. You fix it by buffering the batch and serving from the buffer. The gongdao bei is a two-hundred-gram glass buffer with a spout.

It is also where you read the tea. Glass, not porcelain, because the liquor color is half the information. A pale jade-green Longjing, an amber dancong, a chestnut-black ripe pu'er — you judge the steep by what you see in the jug and correct the next round before it reaches anyone's cup. The pitcher is the dashboard.

The name comes with a story. In the early Ming court, around the reign of the first emperor Hongwu in the late 1300s, a cup was made for the palace called the 九龙公道杯, the Nine Dragons Fairness Cup. It looked like an ordinary wine cup, but hidden in the base was a siphon. Filled to the right line, it behaved. Filled past it, the whole cup drained itself out through a hole in the bottom, and the greedy guest got nothing. The story is usually told as a lesson about moderation: fairness is not only dividing the tea evenly, it is not taking more than your share in the first place.

Chaoshan families still drink this way. Three or four people, one gaiwan, one jug, cups that hold a mouthful. The host pours, the jug travels for a second, and everyone is drinking the same tea at the same second. No one has to be served first and no one has to be served last, because the jug removed the question.

One: pour the entire infusion into the cup before you serve anyone. Not half, not two cups' worth. The whole round goes into the jug, and the jug goes to the cups. Tea left sitting in a gaiwan between pours keeps steeping and turns bitter, so the jug is what buys you the pause.

Two: size it to your pot. A fairness cup should hold at least one full pour from whatever you brew in. A 150ml gaiwan drowns a 120ml jug and leaves you standing there with half a round unserved. Buy 200ml for a 150ml gaiwan and stop thinking about it.

Three: empty it every round. Tea in the jug is off the leaf, so it will not go bitter, but it will go cold and flat within a few minutes. Pour it out, drink it, start the next infusion. A fairness cup that sits full through a whole conversation is a fairness cup of cold tea.

Four: start with glass, upgrade to ceramic later. A plain borosilicate pitcher like this 8oz Sizikato tea sharing cup shows the liquor color and costs almost nothing. If you want something a table will notice, a glazed ceramic gongdao cup like this one holds heat a little longer, at the price of hiding the tea from you. Both work. The glass one teaches you more.

One more thing, for the people already deep in rock tea: you do not need a special fairness cup for Da Hong Pao or Rou Gui. The vessel is variety-agnostic. What changes with the tea is the brewing, and that is a different article — start with the gaiwan guide if you have not.
