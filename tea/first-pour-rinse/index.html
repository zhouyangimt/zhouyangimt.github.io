The First Pour Goes Down the Drain: What a Rinse Actually Does

On 太平路 in 潮州 the tea stalls are the width of a doorway and the whole transaction takes ninety seconds. You sit on a plastic stool. The woman opposite you has a kettle that never switches off, three cups the size of a walnut, and one gaiwan that has been in service long enough that the inside is stained the colour of strong broth. She fills it, drops the lid on, lifts it once to let the steam out, and then tips the entire first pour into the tray. It runs off the edge of the wood and onto the floor. Nobody at the table thinks this is wasteful. It is the part of the process that isn't for you.

I did that gesture wrong for about fifteen years. Not the motion; the motion is easy. I did the reason wrong. Someone told me 洗茶, "washing the tea," and I understood it as hygiene, the dust of a warehouse and whatever had been on the leaf in the field. So I washed everything, including 明前 Longjing, the most expensive thing in my cupboard. I was pouring the best thirty seconds of that leaf into the sink and calling it housekeeping.

The rinse is not a wash. It's a warm-up. And what it warms is not the cup.

Three words for one gesture

Chinese has at least three names for what happens before the first real infusion, and they disagree with each other. 洗茶 says cleaning. 润茶 says moistening. 醒茶 says waking something up. Same hand, same second and a half, three different theories of why.

What's physically going on is closest to the third, and it has nothing to do with dirt. Dry leaf is a bad conductor of heat and a worse conductor of water. A tightly rolled 铁观音 ball, a 357-gram 七子饼 prised apart with a pick, a strip of 岩茶 that has been through three rounds of charcoal roasting: the outside of each piece is wet within a second and the inside is still dry a full infusion later. The rinse wets the surface and drives the air out of the leaf's interior, and you can hear it happen, a small crackle as the ball starts to open. The second pour then extracts from leaf that is already wet and already warm.

It also heats the vessel. A cold porcelain gaiwan pulls heat out of the water on contact. Pour boiling water into a room-temperature bowl and what reaches the leaf can be ten degrees cooler than what left the kettle. Rinsing the bowl first, with the same water you're about to brew with, means the second pour arrives at the temperature you intended. That isn't ritual. That's thermal mass.

Lu Yu counted nine difficulties, and dirt was not one of them

The oldest book on tea in existence is a dry, practical list of what can go wrong. 陆羽, writing in the 760s, in 《茶经·六之饮》: 茶有九难：一曰造，二曰别，三曰器，四曰火，五曰水，六曰炙，七曰末，八曰煮，九曰饮. Nine difficulties: making it, judging it, the vessels, the fire, the water, roasting, grinding, boiling, drinking. Nine. No washing.

When 洗 does turn up in later tea writing it is usually about the vessel, or about 陈气, the stale closed-in smell of tea that spent a damp summer in a jar, or of a roasted oolong drunk too soon after the roast. You rinse those to wake them, the way you decant a wine that has been in bottle too long. It was never a hygiene measure, because for most of the last thousand years the leaf in a Chinese household was fresher than the water.

The hygiene theory is a modern import, and it collides with the arithmetic. Three seconds of boiling water is a terrible detergent: what dissolves in three seconds is a thin film, and most of what people worry about on tea isn't sitting loose on the surface in a form that water lifts off. Meanwhile the compounds that make the first infusion taste of anything are precisely the ones that leave the leaf first. 赵佶 put it plainly in 《大观茶论》 around 1107: 茶有真香，非龙麝可拟. Tea has a true fragrance; nothing you could add, musk or otherwise, imitates it. That fragrance lives in the first thirty seconds of contact with hot water.

So the whole practice is a trade, and the trade only works on tea that can afford it.

Which first pours are worth keeping

There's an old line in tea shops: 头道水，二道茶，三道四道是精华. The first pour is water, the second is tea, the third and fourth are the essence. It's true of most oolong and most puer. It is exactly backwards for green tea.

My rule, after a decade of getting it wrong in both directions: rinse what is compressed, rolled or roasted, and never rinse what is young, loose and green. A 铁观音 ball needs the rinse, or the second pour is spent opening it. A 七子饼 needs it because the cake has sat in paper for years, the outside leaf is dust and the inside is still tight. A charcoal-roasted 大红袍 needs it because the first water comes off the roast rather than the tea. A Dragon Well picked in late March is flat, dry and already open. Its first infusion is the sweetest one it will ever give, and tipping it out is the single most expensive mistake in Chinese tea.

Same for 白毫银针, same for 碧螺春, same for any 黄茶. Nobody in 杭州 rinses a Longjing. Nobody in 潮州 rinses a 单丛 for longer than a second, because the aromatics are the entire point of that leaf.

Five rules that hold

One: rinse hot, or don't bother. Cool water won't open a rolled leaf, so a 40°C rinse is just a wasted infusion. Use the water you're brewing with.

Two: three seconds. Count them. A rinse is a wetting, not a steep. Leave it thirty seconds and you've made a weak cup and thrown it away.

Three: pour it where it does some work. In 潮州 it goes over the cups to scald them before the first real pour. In my kitchen it goes into a small glass pitcher to warm that instead, same idea with less splashing: a warm vessel keeps the second infusion hot all the way down to the last cup.

Four: one rinse for a puer cake, two for a cake you just broke open with a pick, because the torn edges are where the storage smell sits. Two is also right for old white tea that has spent years in a crock.

Five: if the tea is cheap and you don't know where it came from, a rinse won't rescue it. Buy better leaf. There is no rinsing a bad puer into a good one.

The 潮州 stall keeps a small bowl beside the gaiwan called a 茶洗, and everything that comes off the leaf goes in there. When she finally pours the second infusion she says nothing about any of it. The first pour was never a topic of conversation. It exists so that the second one can be right, and after twenty years of drinking tea I've stopped thinking of it as a step at all. It's just the sound the kettle makes before the tea starts.
