Five Cups to Start With: What to Buy First, and What Each One Teaches

Saturday morning in Tianshan Tea City, and the corridor smells of jasmine, wet cardboard and hot water. A woman at one of the stalls is weighing leaf on a brass scale. A man in a North Face jacket is asking her for something good to give his boss. She asks what the boss drinks. He doesn't know. She asks what he drinks. He says, coffee.

She puts a 357-gram cake of ripe puer back on the shelf, takes down a small tin instead, and says the line I've heard from her a dozen times: 先喝,喝完再说买不买. Drink first. We'll talk about buying after.

That exchange is the beginner problem in miniature. You walk in expecting a product and get handed a curriculum. Chinese tea is six categories and a few thousand named teas, and none of it means much until your mouth has a reference point. So here is the list I would hand that man, in the order I would hand it: five cups, picked not because they are the best teas in China but because each one teaches something the next one assumes you already know. Sort by what you learn per gram, not by what costs most.

Why 100 grams and not a cake

Two numbers run your first year. Five grams of leaf in a 110ml gaiwan, three infusions minimum and five to seven if the tea is good. Call it fifteen grams a session, so a hundred grams is about a month of Saturday mornings. A 357-gram puer cake is a two-year commitment to one flavour you may not like yet.

Buy 100g. Write the shop and the date on the bag. When it's empty you'll know whether you want more, and you'll have learned something no sample pack can teach, which is how a tea changes in the six weeks after you open it.

Cup one: Longjing, the clock

Start with a green tea, because green tea is the only category that punishes you for waiting. Longjing from the hills west of Hangzhou is picked in the last days of March and the first days of April, then pressed flat against a hot wok by hand, one leaf at a time. Roughly five kilos of fresh leaf go into one kilo of finished tea, and all of it comes off the bush inside a fortnight.

Brew three grams in a glass at 80°C and watch the leaves drop. The taste sits closer to a smell than a flavour: toasted chestnut, soy milk, and a sweetness that arrives after you swallow. What it teaches is freshness. Drink it in May, then drink the same tea in November from a bag that sat in a cupboard, and you will never again wonder why the shop asks for the pick date before it quotes a price.

Cup two: Tieguanyin, the shape

Anxi Tieguanyin comes rolled into tight green pellets, and the first thing it teaches is that smell and taste are separate instruments. The dry leaf is all orchids. The liquor is buttery and thin next to that perfume, and then the scent climbs back up your nose after you swallow. Chinese drinkers call it 回甘, returning sweetness, and it is the nearest thing tea has to the finish of a good wine.

It also teaches you to brew rather than to steep. Five grams, boiling water, and get out fast: fifteen seconds on the first infusion, five more each round. Leave it a full minute and the same leaf turns bitter, which is a lesson about parameters, not about the tea.

Cup three: Keemun, the forgiveness

Keemun comes from Qimen in Anhui and is the reason English breakfast exists, though England has largely forgotten where it came from. The Chinese-grade leaf is wiry and dark with golden tips, smells of cocoa and dried longan, and brews a red liquor that is malty and soft with an orchid note on the second cup.

It goes third because black tea forgives. It keeps a year in a tin, it doesn't much care whether your water is 90 or 100°C, and it survives four minutes of neglect in the pot. That makes it the tea you actually drink on a Tuesday, and the one that teaches the difference between a tea you admire and a tea you use.

Cup four: Bai Mudan, the patience

White tea is the category beginners misread as weak. Bai Mudan is picked in Fuding as one bud and two leaves, withered in the sun and dried, and it brews pale gold with the taste of hay, honeydew and pear skin. Nothing about it is loud. Drink it expecting oolong intensity and you will decide it is water.

So brew it cool, around 85°C, and leave it in the cup long enough to answer. What it teaches is attention: the flavours are all there at low volume, and finding them is a skill that carries over to everything else. Bai Mudan is also the one tea here that improves while you ignore it. A 2013 cake tastes of dates and old books, and often costs less than this year's leaf.

Cup five: Ripe puer, the floor

Ripe puer is the strangest thing on the list, and the reason to keep going. Since the mid-1970s, factories in Yunnan have sped up the aging of raw puer by piling the leaf warm and damp for 45 to 60 days, a controlled fermentation that turns bitter sheng into something dark, thick and sweet. Good ripe puer smells of damp cellar and dates, pours almost black, and leaves a coating at the back of your throat.

Brew it at a full boil, rinse it twice, and use less leaf than feels right. What it teaches is range. Five cups in, a beginner owns a map: fresh, floral, malty, quiet, dark. Every tea after that gets placed somewhere on it, and the ones that refuse to fit are the ones worth chasing.

Cai Xiang, the Song-dynasty official who wrote the first book devoted entirely to tea, put the skill this way in 1051:

善别茶者,正如相工之视人气色也,隐然察之于内。

One who judges tea well is like a physiognomist reading a face: he perceives what is inside without being told. — Cai Xiang, Record of Tea, 1051

He was writing about tribute tea and the examination culture that grew around it, where officials were graded on their palate. Strip away the court politics and the claim still holds: tasting is comparison, and comparison needs a reference set. Five cups is the smallest set that works.

What to put on the table

One gaiwan brews all five and costs less than the electric kettle already in your kitchen. Add a hundred grams of each tea, a straight-walled glass for the Longjing, and a scale that reads to a gram. A plain white porcelain 110ml gaiwan is the one I reach for most, since you want to see the liquor, not the pot. The gaiwan guide covers the three-finger grip and the pour.

If buying five teas at once feels like too much, a six-type sampler will get you the map in a month, though it won't teach you what a full hundred grams does over six weeks. Keep the bags small and sealed; the storage guide explains why a big tin you open every week is worse than three small ones.

I still have the notebook from my first year, with five columns and a line for every session: grams, water temperature, seconds, and a word for the finish. Most of the words are wrong. The habit was right. Five cups won't make anyone an expert, but they will make the sixth cup a comparison instead of a guess, and that is the whole difference between drinking tea and buying it.

The man in the jacket left with a hundred grams of Keemun and a phone number written on the bag. If you were standing at that counter, which of the five would you ask for first? Most people say black tea, because it is the one they already recognise. The better answer is usually the one you are least sure about.
