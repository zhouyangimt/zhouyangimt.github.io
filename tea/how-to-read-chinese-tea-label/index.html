The Words on the Tin: How to Read a Chinese Tea Label

The tin was gold, roughly the size of a paperback, and it carried eleven separate pieces of information on the front. 特级 sat at the top in the largest type, printed red. Underneath it, 明前. Then a line of smaller characters, a QR code, a phone number, and at the bottom, in type you needed a magnifier for, a code beginning with SC.

Maliandao, the tea street in Beijing, second week of November, shops running a kilometre along the south side of the road. I had asked for something to keep at the office. The owner set this tin on the counter and named a price. I asked what 特级 meant, exactly. She said: good grade. I pointed at the tin beside it, also 特级, forty percent cheaper. She said: that is a different tea.

She was right, and that is the entire difficulty. A Chinese tea label is a legal document stapled to an advertisement, and both halves are set in the same font at the same weight. 特级 on one tin and 特级 on another are not the same sentence, because the word only exists inside the rulebook for one specific tea. Learn which half of the label you are reading and the tin stops being mysterious.

Nine fields the law requires, and the one that grades the tea

Start with what is compulsory. China's national food labelling standard, GB 7718-2011, applies to every prepackaged food sold in the country, tea included, and it names the fields that must appear:

预包装食品标签应当标明：食品名称、配料表、净含量和规格、生产者或经销者的名称、地址和联系方式、生产日期和保质期、贮存条件、食品生产许可证编号、产品标准代号。

GB 7718-2011《食品安全国家标准 预包装食品标签通则》, §4.1, abridged, my translation: name, ingredients, net weight, producer or distributor with address and contact, production date and shelf life, storage conditions, food production licence number, product standard code.

Eight of those nine fields are housekeeping. They tell you who to sue and when the tea goes stale. The ninth, 产品标准代号, is the only one that tells you what the tea is supposed to be, because it names the document the tea was graded against.

The numbers are not secret. GB/T 18650 is the standard for 龙井茶, Longjing. GB/T 22111 is 普洱茶, pu'er. GB/T 14456.1 covers green tea in general. When a tin prints one of these, the words 特级、一级、二级 above it are pinned to a published table of leaf shape, colour, aroma and liquor, and a inspector can be sent to check. When the tin prints nothing, or prints a code starting with Q/, the grade is the seller's own opinion written in a house standard. That is not automatically bad tea. It is unverified tea, and it should be priced like it.

The 食品生产许可证 number, the SC code, is worth thirty seconds of your time. It runs fourteen digits. The first three are the food category, and for tea the code is 1401. The next two are the province of the licence holder. Here is the honest catch: that province belongs to whoever packed the tin, not to whoever grew the leaf. A Hangzhou trading company can legally pack Fujian leaf, so a Zhejiang province code does not prove 西湖 origin. What the number does prove is that the packer is a licensed food producer, which is more than you can say for a paper bag at a tourist market.

Then there is 特级 itself, which is where most people go wrong. It is a comparative grade inside one product standard, not a national rank. A 特级 Longjing is judged on flatness, straightness, the ratio of bud to leaf, and whether the dry leaf smells of chestnut rather than hay. A 特级 Keemun is judged on entirely different things. The two 特级 are not comparable, and paying a premium because one tin says 特级 and the other says 一级 means you are buying a number without its rulebook. In Longjing, honestly, a well-made 一级 often drinks better than a thin 特级: more grip in the mouth, a longer finish, the chestnut note still there. 特级 buys you delicacy and a shorter finish. Some of us want the grip.

The two words that mean nothing at all

明前 is not a grade and no standard defines it. It means picked before Qingming, the tomb-sweeping festival on 4 or 5 April, and it is a date, not a quality. On a spring green tea the date is genuinely useful, because early leaf grew slowly through cold weather and carries more amino acids and fewer bitter catechins than leaf picked three weeks later in full sun. On a roasted oolong picked in late April, or a pu'er cake pressed in autumn, 明前 is decoration. It sells the memory of spring to someone buying in November.

古树, old tree, is the other one. It appears on pu'er wrappers everywhere and GB/T 22111 gives it no definition: no age threshold, no test, no consequence for printing it on a cake from ten-year-old bushes. Same for 野生 and 乔木王. These are adjectives, and adjectives are free.

What the leaf itself says is harder to fake. A cake that genuinely came off old trees usually shows thick, elastic leaves with visible vein structure after brewing, and it keeps giving sweet water for eight or ten infusions instead of collapsing at four. You can verify that at home with a gaiwan and no chemistry set, which is more than you can do with a printed word.

Why China never wrote one national ladder

India grades tea with a trade alphabet, FTGFOP and its relatives, one vocabulary for the whole country. China went the other way, and the reason is scale. There are thousands of named teas here, and nearly every producing county spent centuries defending its own name. A single national ladder would have forced 西湖龙井 and 六安瓜片 onto the same ruler, and neither county would have accepted it.

So the standards arrived product by product, written after the reputation rather than before it. The 2008 revision of GB/T 18650 did something quietly enormous for Longjing: it wrote the three production zones, 西湖, 钱塘 and 越州, into a national document. Before that split was legal text, 西湖龙井 was a phrase any packer could print. After it, a tin either has the paperwork for the West Lake zone or it does not, and the price difference between the zones runs to a factor of several. That is what a standard code is: a fence around a name.

Thirty seconds at the counter

Run the label in this order and skip everything else.

One. Find 产品标准代号. GB/T plus five digits means a real rulebook. Q/ means the company grades itself.

Two. Read 配料表. It should say 茶 and nothing else. 食用香精 means flavoured tea wearing a famous name; 茉莉花 is legitimate, because jasmine is scented with real flowers.

Three. Look for 生产日期, and be suspicious of a 包装日期 stamped in large type instead. Packing date tells you when a bag was filled, not when a leaf was picked.

Four. Check 贮存条件. A spring green tea that says 冷藏 and 避光 was handled by someone who expects you to taste it. A green tea labelled 常温 in July is tired already.

Five. Decode the first five digits of the SC number. 1401 plus a province is a licensed packer, and now you know where the tin was filled.

Then price the code, not the adjectives. Once you have a tin you trust, the container matters more than people admit: a hundred-gram enamel caddy with a rubber-seated lid keeps a green tea crisp for months, where the original gold tin, once opened, lets in air every time you wrestle the lid off. The storage guide covers the four things that kill leaf; if you are still choosing what to put in the caddy, start with the six-bag list.

The woman in Maliandao was not being evasive. She was telling me that 特级 is a word with a home address, and that two tins sharing it were standing in different countries. Half the label is law. The other half is a story someone wrote to sell the law. Knowing which half you are holding is most of what buying tea in China requires.

I still get it wrong. Last month I bought a 明前 green in a shop that had no standard code printed anywhere, because the tin was beautiful and the shop smelled right. It tasted of nothing by the third infusion. I knew exactly what I was doing and did it anyway.
