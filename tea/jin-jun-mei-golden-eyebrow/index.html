Jin Jun Mei: The Black Tea Made Only of Buds

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

The pickers left before the sun, because the tea that pays for the whole year exists for only about a week, and only in the first cold hours of it. I followed a woman named Zhang out of Tongmu village in the dark, up a path I could not see, my flashlight swinging across wet stone and the black shapes of tea bushes. At the top of the slope, with the Wuyi peaks breathing cold down the valley, she bent over a bush and showed me what she was after. A single bud, closed tight as a matchstick, wrapped in a fine gold down. Not a leaf. A bud. Six hours of numb fingers would fill a cloth bag no bigger than a sandwich.

That bud is Jin Jun Mei — Golden Eyebrow — the most expensive black tea in China, made from nothing but the tip of the shoot. It was invented in 2005, which makes it younger than my first job, and it comes from the same village, the same family, and the same bushes that created Lapsang Souchong four hundred years earlier. The first black tea on Earth was an accident of fire and smoke. This one is an act of almost absurd restraint.

Fifty Thousand Buds

The math is the first thing to understand. To make one jin — about five hundred grams — of finished Jin Jun Mei takes between fifty and sixty thousand individual buds, every one picked by hand before the bud opens, in the brief window before Qingming in early April. A skilled picker manages a few thousand buds a day. Run the numbers and you understand the price before you ever taste the tea: there is no machine, no shortcut, no cheaper way to gather sixty thousand closed buds off a mountainside.

The surprising part is how simple the recipe is. In 2005 a tea maker named Liang Junde — a member of the Jiang family, the lineage that has made Lapsang Souchong in Tongmu for four centuries — was asked to create a top-grade gift tea. He took the same local small-leaf bushes and did two things: he used only the bud, and he left out the smoke. Lapsang Souchong is finished over smoldering pine, which is where its campfire comes from. Jin Jun Mei is dried gently, so the leaf keeps its natural honey and floral character. The result is a black tea with none of black tea's usual muscle. No smoke, no tannic grip. Just sweetness, turned up as far as it will go.

The name is a three-part promise. Jin (金), gold, for the golden down on every bud and the amber it pours. Jun (骏), a fine horse — a character borrowed from Liang Junde's own name. Mei (眉), eyebrow, for the slender curl of the dried leaf. Golden Eyebrow. I spent thirteen years in software, and the difference between a good system and a great one is almost always what you choose to leave out. Jin Jun Mei is that lesson steeped in a cup: same village, same family, same bush as Lapsang Souchong — and the only real change is that someone decided to strip away almost everything and keep the single essential thing.

What you actually taste: the liquor pours a clear, bright amber, the color of late-afternoon honey. The aroma is roasted sweet potato and honey, with something floral sitting just behind it — orchid, or osmanthus. The first sip is silky, almost creamy, with no astringency anywhere in it. Then the hui gan arrives — the returning sweetness that rises in your throat after you swallow, the way the finish of a good wine does — and it stays there a long time. It does not taste like a black tea. It tastes like what a black tea would be if it had nothing left to prove.

The Gift

In China, Jin Jun Mei matters as much as a gesture as a drink. It landed in 2005 into a country that had just discovered luxury, and it was perfectly timed to become the thing you gave to someone who mattered — a boss, a father-in-law, a partner you wanted to impress. A few grams in a lacquered box said, quietly, what no bottle of baijiu could. Within a few years the name had become shorthand for "expensive," which is also when the counterfeits arrived. Cheap black tea got relabeled Jin Jun Mei everywhere, until the word came to promise money more than quality. The flattery and the tragedy are the same: a tea invented yesterday, by tea's standards, already carries a thousand years of social weight.

How to Brew It (and How Not to Get Burned Buying It)

Three rules for brewing, and one honest warning about buying.

First, gentler water than you'd use for most black tea. Around 90°C, never a rolling boil — boiling water cooks the honey right out of those fragile buds. A variable-temperature kettle removes the guesswork. Second, give the buds room to show off. Four grams in a gaiwan or a glass pot, ten-second steeps, and watch the golden tips uncurl through six or eight rounds, every one of them sweet. A porcelain gaiwan is the traditional tool (here's how to use one), and a glass pot is half the pleasure, because these buds are too pretty to hide. Third, don't add anything. No milk, no sugar, no lemon. This tea is already dessert.

The warning: real Tongmu Jin Jun Mei is a specialty-shop tea, and it's genuinely hard to find on Amazon or any Western shelf. What's labeled "Jin Jun Mei" there is usually a looser, lower-grade leaf that won't show you why the name became famous. When you shop, look for golden tips threaded through dark leaves, a sweet-potato-and-honey aroma, and a price that makes you flinch. If it's cheap, it isn't Jin Jun Mei. Buy the best bud-only tea you can afford from a shop that can tell you where it came from. The teaware, at least, is something you'll use every day no matter what.
