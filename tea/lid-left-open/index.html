The Lid Left Open: How a Teapot Asks for Water

Seven-forty on a Sunday. The pot on our table was down to its last dark inch. 四叔 did not lift his head, did not look for anyone, did not say a word. He took the lid off the pot, tipped it until it stood up against the rim on its edge, and put his hand back under the table. Nobody at the table reacted. The woman behind me kept arguing about a wedding seating chart. A trolley of steamed dumplings went past under a steel dome.

Ninety seconds later a waiter in a white shirt arrived with a kettle and filled the pot from a height, water going in as one unbroken thread. Then he was gone, and 四叔 poured for the whole table without ever having asked for anything.

That was the third floor of a teahouse on 第十甫路, in a room loud enough that a normal request would have needed repeating. And this is the part I want to explain, because it took me years to see it: in Guangzhou the lid of a teapot is a switch. Nothing about it is decorative. It has three positions, waiters read those positions off the table as they walk, and a Cantonese breakfast runs its entire service on that one piece of information.

Three positions, one bit of information

Down means leave me alone. I am still drinking this pot, and the leaves in it are still working.

Open means I need water. You lift the lid and stand it on its edge against the mouth of the pot, or prop it half off, so that from three metres away the pot is no longer a closed shape.

Upside down, or laid flat on the table beside the pot, means take it away. That pot is finished and so are we.

I spent thirteen years writing code before I started writing about tea, and the nearest thing I know to this is a flag on a shared board. The knock on the table that thanks a person for pouring travels by sound: it works at arm's length, it needs the other person looking roughly your way, and it is gone the instant it happens. The lid is the opposite kind of signal. It persists. A waiter carrying a kettle between forty tables does not have to be near you, does not have to look at your face and does not have to remember anything. He scans the lids on his way past and stops where the state has changed. In a room where every pot is the same white porcelain shape, the open one is the only object that breaks the pattern.

Which is why it works across a room this loud. The aisles are narrow, the staff walk with their eyes on the tables rather than on the customers, and a raised hand gets lost in the traffic of a trolley. A lid sticking up does not get lost.

What the refill actually delivers is not volume. It is temperature. Cantonese teahouses brew 普洱, 水仙, 铁观音 — leaves built to give up their flavour to water close to a hundred degrees, and to keep giving for six, eight, ten infusions. A pot that has been sitting for twenty minutes is holding water at maybe eighty. Refill number three tastes thin and slightly sweet in the wrong way, and most people blame the leaves instead of the water. 陆羽 wrote the diagnosis down twelve hundred years ago, in the chapter on boiling:

其沸如鱼目，微有声，为一沸。缘边如涌泉连珠，为二沸。腾波鼓浪，为三沸。已上，水老不可食也。

《茶经·五之煮》, my translation: when it boils in bubbles the size of fish eyes, with a faint sound, that is the first boil. When the edges rise like a spring of linked pearls, that is the second. When it surges and breaks into waves, that is the third. Past that, the water is old and cannot be used.

Good teahouses understand the second half of that sentence: you cannot fix a flat pot with water that has been sitting in a thermos. The kettles come off a rolling boil or they do not come at all, which is also why the man with the kettle walks so fast.

Why the customer can demand this

Here is the part that took me longest to understand, because it explains the confidence of the gesture. In Guangzhou you pay 茶位, a seat-and-tea charge, per person, before you eat anything. It is on the bill in every teahouse in the city, it costs a few yuan, and it buys you the seat, the pot, the leaves and unlimited hot water. So the open lid is not a favour being requested from a busy man. It is a claim on something already purchased, which is exactly why nobody at that table looked up, checked whether the waiter seemed available, or apologised for the interruption.

The building itself carries the same logic. Guangzhou calls these places 茶楼 — tea buildings — and the name is not poetry. The old 茶寮 and 茶室 were single-storey rooms for a quick cup. When 陶陶居 opened on 第十甫路 in 1880 and 莲香楼 followed in 1889, part of the appeal was that you had to climb: three storeys, high ceilings, tall windows, a floor for the morning trade and a floor for the afternoon. The order was 一盅两件, one pot and two dim sum, and the pot was assumed to be a long engagement rather than a quick drink.

The north never built the same grammar, and it is not a matter of manners. In Beijing the lid is a tool, not a switch. You drink from a 盖碗, a bowl with a lid and a saucer, and the lid exists to be used: you draw it across the surface to push the foam aside and hold the leaves back from your mouth. A tool that is in your hand every thirty seconds cannot also be a status flag, because it is never in a resting position long enough to mean anything. And in the old Beijing teahouse the house rules were printed and hung rather than performed, a fact 老舍 put at the centre of his 1957 play 茶馆, where the four characters pasted on the wall of 王利发's shop are 莫谈国事 — no discussion of state affairs. Written notice in the north, wordless gesture in the south. Same drink, two different grammars.

Six rules, if you want to do it properly

One. Wait until the pot is nearly empty. Topping up a half-full pot is the one mistake that cannot be undone: you stretch the same leaves through more water and the next two infusions taste like a memory of tea. Drain it first.

Two. Stand the lid up; do not nudge it. A lid shifted one centimetre reads as closed from four tables away. It has to break the outline of the pot.

Three. Never knock on the pot to get attention. Drumming the body is a hurry signal, and in a teahouse it is the one move guaranteed to make your service slower. The lid is a statement about your pot; knocking is a statement about the staff.

Four. Do not flag a pot that is still full. Twice at one table and the floor staff start checking on you with a particular expression, the way a waiter checks on a customer who has asked for the bill three times.

Five. When you are finished, put the lid down — inverted, or flat on the table beside the pot. Shops differ on which one their staff are trained to read, so watch the next table for half a minute before you commit.

Six. Outside Guangdong, don't. A propped lid in Beijing gets you a puzzled look and nothing else, because the waiter has no reason to have ever learned the code. In Shanghai, where I grew up, you raise two fingers at the floor staff and they come. Different city, different interface, same purpose.

At home the signal is unnecessary, and that is the one place I would spend money. A glass teapot shows you the water line and the colour of the liquor at a glance, so the pot never reaches the state where you need to flag it, and you can watch a rolled oolong open over four infusions without lifting the lid at all. For the temperature half of the argument, the piece on kettles covers why a pot that holds heat badly ruins the third pour, and if you prefer brewing in a bowl the gaiwan grip explains where the lid goes when your hand is the thing holding it.

What I like about the whole arrangement is that it is unglamorous. Nobody teaches it. There is no ceremony, no speech, no correct angle of the elbow. It is a piece of local engineering that solved a real problem — forty tables, one waiter, no way to hear anybody — and then quietly became manners. My mother, who has lived her whole life in Shanghai, still finds it theatrical and asks why people can't simply say 加水. They can. It's just that in that room, with that kettle going past, a lid does it faster.
