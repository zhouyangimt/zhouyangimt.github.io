Shou Mei: Longevity Eyebrow, the Coarse Leaf That Ages Into Treasure

The same bushes get picked three times, and the third pass is not a careful one. Late March, buds alone: Silver Needle. Two weeks later, one bud and two leaves: Bai Mudan. Then spring runs out. In early May, or again in September after the White Dew solar term, the pickers walk the rows one last time and take what is left — the wide mature leaf, the woody stem, the shoot that opened days ago and has gone leathery.

Nobody sorts that pass. The leaf goes onto bamboo trays in thick layers, withers in the sun, finishes over low heat, and ends up in a sack. In the hand it looks like a sweep of a hayloft floor: grey-green, brittle, pale stems sticking out — and it is the cheapest white tea Fujian makes.

The tea is 寿眉, Shou Mei, “Longevity Eyebrow.” The name describes the finished leaf: dried, it curls into a thin crescent, and a tray of it looks like a heap of eyebrows. The grades before it took the good material; Shou Mei is what they left behind. Which is exactly why it is the most interesting of the three — it is the one that time improves.

What the Cheap Grade Is Made Of

Shou Mei sits at the bottom of the white tea ladder by picking grade, not by process. The recipe is Silver Needle’s: wither, dry, no kill-green firing, no rolling, no roast. Silver Needle is one bud, Bai Mudan a bud and two leaves, Shou Mei mature leaf and stem with almost no bud at all.

Two names need sorting out, because shops treat them as interchangeable. 贡眉, Gong Mei — “Tribute Eyebrow” — was historically the finer grade, picked from the small-leaf local bush (菜茶, cai cha) rather than the big-leaf cultivars Shou Mei comes from. Much of what sells as Gong Mei today is Shou Mei under a better label. If a seller wants a premium for the word, ask which bush it came from.

Coarse leaf is not worse leaf, it is different leaf. Mature leaves carry more catechins, which give grip and structure, and more pectin and soluble sugar, which give weight in the mouth. The stems look like a defect; they are where the sweetness lives, being high in soluble sugars and amino acids, the same reason a stem-heavy green tea tastes rounder than a bud-only one. What Shou Mei loses is theanine and the fine floral top notes that come with it. What it gains is body, and ten or twelve steeps where a Silver Needle gives four.

Then there is the aging, which turned a farmhouse tea into a collector’s tea. White tea is barely processed, so its enzymes stay intact and keep changing in storage. Fresh hay and melon fade over the years; 枣香, jujube — the dried red date — arrives, plus medicinal herbs, old wood, honey. Fujian researchers have measured flavonoids climbing as white tea ages, the footing under the local claim that old white tea is medicine. Stay skeptical of the medicine, confident about the taste. A well-stored cake from ten years ago is not a stronger young tea. It is a different drink.

I spent thirteen years shipping software, and the thing that took me longest to learn is that the unglamorous component is usually the one holding the system up. Shou Mei is that idea in a sack: the part of the harvest nobody bids for, quietly improving while the prized buds go flat.

What it actually tastes like. The dry leaf smells of hay, autumn leaves, sweet dry wood. Young Shou Mei brews a pale gold that many people find underwhelming: hay, a little melon rind, a light grip at the sides of the tongue, then 回甘 — huigan, the returning sweetness. Think of the finish of a good wine: nothing sugary on the way in, but something sweet keeps arriving a minute after you swallow. From five years on the liquor has gone amber to copper, the texture thickens toward syrup, and the date-and-herb notes arrive. By then it is not a tea you sip but a tea you drink by the pot.

The Tea on the Dim Sum Table

Order dim sum in Hong Kong or old Guangzhou and the first question is which tea. 寿眉 is always one of the names called out, and usually the cheapest. The pot arrives dark and cloudy, refilled without anyone asking, and it is not there to be admired. It is there to cut through a steamed pork bun and keep a table talking for two hours: mild, no bitterness when it sits too long, easy on the stomach.

The other place Shou Mei lived was the Chinese medicine shop: sold by the paper bag from 药铺 — apothecaries — beside the dried tangerine peel, and shipped out to Chinese communities in Malaya and Singapore in wooden chests, because it traveled well and cost little. Shou Mei was the household tea and the export tea, and that it ages better than its betters is the joke history played on grading.

How to Brew It, and How to Buy It

Shou Mei is the most forgiving tea in the white family, and the usual mistake is handling it too gently. Three rules.

One: for young leaf, more leaf and hotter water. Silver Needle wants 85°C and restraint. Shou Mei wants 95°C and 5g per 100ml, first steep twenty seconds, then ten more each round. If the cup comes out bitter, your steep was too long, not your water too hot. A variable-temperature kettle gets you to 95°C without guessing.

Two: for old leaf, boil it. Aged Shou Mei is one of the few Chinese teas you are supposed to cook. Five grams into 500–600ml, bring it to a simmer, hold it there five to eight minutes, drink it as it cools. Or gaiwan the leaf for three or four steeps first, then move the spent leaves into a pot of water and simmer that — the standard move in Guangdong and Fujian, and one more reason to keep a gaiwan around. Add a strip of dried tangerine peel for the household version.

Three: buy the year, not the adjective. Fresh Shou Mei is the cheapest door into white tea, often half the price of Silver Needle. Look for a place name (Fuding, Zhenghe) and a harvest year on the bag. Yulongsheng’s Fuding loose leaf is a straightforward version of that grade: whole leaf, sun-dried, and it brews the hay-and-melon cup described above rather than a dusty one. For the other end of the story, buy a cake with the year pressed into the paper. This 2016 Fuding cake has had a decade to go dark and date-sweet.

Storage matters more here than anywhere else on the shelf, because you are buying a transformation rather than a Tuesday. Airtight, dark, away from the kitchen: white tea takes on the smell of whatever shares its shelf. Then leave it alone. The cheapest tea on the shelf is also the one most likely to end up worth more than you paid for it. All you have to do is forget about it.
