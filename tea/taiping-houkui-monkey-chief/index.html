Taiping Houkui: The Monkey Chief of Green Tea

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

The first time someone handed me Taiping Houkui, I thought they were giving me a pressed flower, not tea. Each leaf lay flat on the white paper, a full hand-width long — deep green, straight as a blade, with a pale lattice of veins pressed through it like a watermark. It looked like something from a Victorian botanist's specimen drawer, not a thing you drop into hot water. Then I caught the smell: orchid, wet stone, and a faint sweetness like cold rice.

I was in Taiping County, at the foot of the Huangshan range in Anhui, in the first week of April. The mist had come down overnight and never left; the tea gardens climbed the slopes in green terraces that vanished into cloud thirty meters up. A farmer's wife showed me how they pick it — not the delicate bud-and-tip pinch of most green teas, but whole sprigs, two big leaves and a bud, snapped off and laid flat in a basket like roof tiles.

Taiping Houkui — "Monkey Chief" — is the largest-leaf green tea in China, and the only one whose leaves are pressed flat between sheets of cloth. Everything about it refuses to behave the way green tea is supposed to.

Big Leaves, Flat Pressed, Slow Release

The leaves start big because of the cultivar. Taiping Houkui only comes from one local line called Shidaye — "Persimmon Big Leaf," named because the leaves grow as broad as persimmon leaves — grown around a handful of villages in Taiping. Most green teas are built on tenderness: the smaller the bud, the more precious. Taiping Houkui is built on size. The pick is called "two knives and one spear" (两刀一枪): two flat leaves like paired blades, with a single bud standing between them like a spear point.

Processing keeps that shape instead of destroying it. Pluck, wither, kill-green — so far it's just green tea. Then comes the step no other tea does: the leaves are laid out one at a time on a screen, covered with cloth, and pressed under a roller until every leaf is perfectly flat and perfectly straight. No curling, no rolling into pearls. There's a proverb about it — "Monkey Chief has two pointed ends; it doesn't scatter, doesn't curl, doesn't roll its edges." The pressing is also what stamps that pale grid into the leaf, the "red thread" (红丝线) that collectors look for: a watermark of honest processing.

Here's the part that lit up my programmer brain. A giant leaf, pressed flat, gives up its contents slowly — so the same steep that would turn a delicate bud tea bitter stays sweet pour after pour. Big surface area, slow release. You don't have to babysit it. I spent thirteen years debugging systems where the whole trick was controlling how fast things let go; Taiping Houkui does it with a leaf and a roller.

The taste: the liquor is pale, nearly colorless at first, deepening to a soft apricot-yellow. It smells like orchid and fresh-cut grass. The first sip is sweet and clean — less vegetal than most green teas, closer to chestnut with a faint floral note — and there's almost no bitterness. Then the finish: a long, cool sweetness that climbs back up the throat and stays. Chinese drinkers have a word for it: "猴韵," the monkey's lingering charm. Think of it like the finish of a good wine, the part that's still there after the glass is empty.

The Monkey, the Gold Medal, and the Tiny Mountain

The name is half legend. The story goes that a Qing-dynasty tea farmer watched wild monkeys pick leaves from cliffs too steep for any man, and named the tea for their leader, the Monkey Chief. Believe it or not — the truth is less romantic but sharper. "魁" doesn't just mean chief; it means first, the best of its class. And that's what Taiping Houkui has claimed since 1915, when it took a gold medal at the Panama-Pacific Exposition in San Francisco and came home with a reputation it's been living on ever since.

The real weight of the tea is smaller than any legend. The authentic growing area is tiny — one core village and its neighbors, a few square kilometers of mountainside. Everything else sold as "Houkui" is grown somewhere else and pressed flat to fake it. That scarcity is why it sits among the most expensive green teas in China, and why a box of it is a serious gift. You don't give someone Taiping Houkui to be polite. You give it to say: I know what the good stuff is, and so do you.

How to Brew It, and How Not to Get Burned

Brew it in glass, and stand the leaves up. Use a tall glass, drop the flat leaves in, and pour water down the side — the leaves will rise onto their ends and slowly unfurl, and watching that is half the pleasure. Because the leaves are thick, they can take slightly hotter water than other green teas: 85°C, not 80. A variable-temperature kettle removes the guesswork; otherwise, boil and let it sit a minute and a half.

Three rules. First, use a tall vessel, not a cramped gaiwan — these leaves need room to stand; a clear glass teapot works, or any tall glass you already own. Second, go generous on the leaf — about three grams per 200ml — and steep one to two minutes, then refill. You'll get three or four clean pours, and the second is usually the best. Third, if it tastes bitter, the fault is your water, not the tea. Taiping Houkui is the hardest green tea to ruin.

Where to start: look for flat, uniform, blade-shaped leaves — two leaves and a bud, perfectly straight, with that pale grid pressed through them. Real core-area Houkui is genuinely rare outside China and heavily counterfeited on Amazon. If a listing names the region and the flat two-leaves-one-bud shape, buy it. For a first cup, this Taiping Houkui green tea is a modest, honest place to begin — expect a pale, sweet, orchid-scented brew, and judge it by whether the finish lingers.
