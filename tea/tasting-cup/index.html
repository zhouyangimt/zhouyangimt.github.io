The Tasting Cup: Why Chinese Tea Is Served in a Thimble

The cup arrives empty, and that is the part I had to learn. It was a teahouse in Chaozhou, in a back room that smelled of roasted oolong and wet stone. The host filled a small porcelain cup no bigger than a walnut, set it in front of me, then reached over and took it back. She poured the tea into a second cup, tall and narrow, handed me that one, and waited while I sniffed it like a wine glass. Then she took that away too and slid the first cup back in front of me. The tea inside had gone from scalding to warm. "闻香," she said. Smell the aroma. The cup that had held the tea was the interesting one now, precisely because it was empty.

That is the tasting cup, 品茗杯, pǐnmíng bēi. It is the smallest vessel on any Chinese tea table, often thirty to sixty millilitres, smaller than a shot glass, and it looks like an accident of history: a cup someone forgot to make bigger. It is not. The size, the thin wall, the flared lip, and the fact that it gets passed under your nose after the tea is gone, are all doing a job.

The first thing visitors ask is why the cup is so small. The honest answer is that gongfu tea is not drunk, it is tasted, and six or seven infusions from one leaf means six or seven little servings rather than one big mug. You cannot push half a litre through eight rounds without drowning the leaf. The cup is a metronome. It sets the pace of the conversation: a mouthful, a pause, another mouthful.

The second answer is temperature. A cup that holds a mouthful cools to the temperature where tea actually tastes of something, call it fifty to sixty degrees, in about ninety seconds. A big mug is still too hot after five minutes, then goes cold in your hands. The aroma compounds, the volatiles that carry the orchid and the roast and the honey, sit concentrated in the air just above a cooling cup. Too hot and you burn your nose; too cold and they have already left. The small cup is a timer set for the exact window when the tea is worth smelling.

Shape does the rest. A narrow, tall cup funnels aroma toward your face, which is why the 闻香杯, the aroma cup, is built like a chimney. A wide, shallow tasting cup sends the liquor to the front of your tongue first, where sweetness registers. The classic 品茗杯 flares at the lip so the tea spreads before it reaches your mouth.

A thin porcelain wall is a heatsink. I spent thirteen years at Microsoft, Google and NVIDIA, and I know a thermal path when I see one. Heat leaves the tea through the wall fast, which sounds like a flaw and is the whole trick: the wall pulls the liquor down to smelling temperature in the time it takes to pass the cups around the table.

Material matters less than people think, with one exception. White porcelain is the neutral choice: it takes no flavour, and its pale interior is a colour chart for the liquor, which is how you judge whether you let it steep two seconds too long. Glass shows the leaves dancing but loses heat quickly. Celadon and crackle glaze are for the eye. Yixing clay, the purple sand of the famous teapots, is the one to avoid in a cup. It seasons, and a cup that still remembers yesterday's pu'er will argue with today's Longjing.

Drinking tea in China runs on two different verbs. 饮, yǐn, is to drink, to quench thirst, the thing you do with water on a hot day. 品, pǐn, is to taste: to weigh, to judge, to slow down. The character 品 is three mouths stacked on top of each other, and the old rule follows the writing. A proper tasting takes three sips. The first is too hot to judge. The second is where the flavour opens. By the third you know what you are drinking. The little cup does not just hold less tea. It gives you permission to stop.

Then there is the empty-cup habit, strongest in Taiwan and the Chaoshan south. After you drink you do not set the cup down and move on. You tip it toward your nose, breathe, and try to name what is left behind: the toast, the stone fruit, the wet rock. A tea that smells better in the empty cup than in the full one is usually a good roast, because the aromatics that cling after the liquid is gone are the expensive ones. People buy perfume to smell like this. Here it is the leftover scent of a two-dollar cup of tea.

One piece of etiquette comes with the cup. When someone pours for you, you tap two fingers on the table. The story says a Qing emperor once travelled in disguise among his officials and poured the tea himself. Unable to kowtow in public without giving him away, they tapped bent fingers on the table in place of a bow. It survived as a silent thank-you, the tea-table equivalent of a nod.

One: buy six, not one. Tasting cups are social objects and they live in sets. Six matched cups cover the round from a 150ml gaiwan and let you seat a table without counting.

Two: white inside, always. Whatever the outside looks like, the inside should be pale so the liquor colour reads. A dark interior is a black box. You would be tasting blind.

Three: keep them small. Thirty to sixty millilitres. If you are refilling one cup for the whole table, it is too big; if your fingers cannot hold it, it is too small.

Four: warm it first. A cold cup drops the tea ten degrees in the first second. Rinse each cup with hot water and tip it out right before you pour.
