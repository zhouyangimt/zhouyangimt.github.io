The Tea Pet: The One Thing Lu Yu Never Listed

My friend Wei keeps his tea table against the window of a lane house in Hongkou, and in January that window is the only warm thing in the room. There is a toad on the tray. Purple clay, about the size of a fist, back covered in bumps, mouth open in something between a grin and a cough. Before the first infusion he rinses the pot with hot water and tips the rinse over the toad's head, and the water runs down its back and into the tray.

Nine years, he said, when I asked how old it was. It cost him forty yuan. When he bought it, it was the same colour as the pot next to it. Now the toad is two shades darker and the patch where the water always lands has gone smooth and half-shiny, the way a stone in a river goes smooth.

A tea pet, 茶宠, is the only piece of teaware that does nothing. It holds no water, strains no leaf, measures nothing. What it does is keep a record. Every session leaves a film of tea on the clay, the film oxidises and darkens, and after a few hundred sessions the surface has a skin that no factory can print. That is the whole object: a clock you can hold in one hand.

The canon stops at twenty-four

Lu Yu finished The Classic of Tea around 780 and he was not a man who left things out. Chapter four itemises the vessels one by one, from the brazier to the basket that carries the whole set, and chapter nine closes the account with a line that has aged into a proverb:

但城邑之中，王公之门，二十四器阙一，则茶废矣。

But inside a walled city, in the house of a prince, if one of the twenty-four vessels is missing, the tea is spoiled. — The Classic of Tea, chapter nine

Twenty-four. Not twenty-five. You can go through the rest of the canon too: Cai Xiang's Record of Tea in the eleventh century, Emperor Huizong's Treatise on Tea in 1107, Xu Cishu's Notes on Tea in 1597. None of them has a tea pet. The word does not appear. Whatever this object is, it is not classical, and nobody in the tea world pretends otherwise.

The reason is a change in scale. The tea those books describe was whisked in a bowl, in quantities that needed a whole table of equipment and a servant to work it. Small-pot brewing, the gongfu method where a hundred millilitres of water meets seven grams of leaf, took shape in southern Fujian and around Chaozhou much later, and the pet arrives later still. As a mass habit it is a child of the 1980s, when Yixing clay came back after the collectivist decades and people started treating a teapot as something to be raised rather than used. The craft underneath it is older: Yixing zisha pottery firing went onto China's first national list of intangible cultural heritage in 2006.

What raising actually means

Zisha is fired unglazed, somewhere around 1,100 to 1,200 degrees, which leaves it with the open, slightly porous structure the potters call double-pore. Tea gets into that surface. It does not wash off, it sits there, oxidises, and slowly turns from a stain into a sheen. The shine on a well-kept pet is not wax and not a glaze. It is tea solids, a little oil from your hands, and years of a soft cloth.

You can see the schedule of it on any old pet. The base darkens first, because that is where the water pools and sits. The high points of the carving stay lighter longest, because they are the parts your thumb keeps wiping. A toad that has been fed for a decade looks like a toad that has been outdoors for a decade: warmer, deeper, and slightly uneven in a way that reads as age rather than damage.

Which is why I have no patience for the colour-changing ones. Those are resin or glazed ceramic loaded with thermochromic pigment, and they flip from brown to green the moment 45-degree water hits them, then flip back when they cool. It is a good trick and children love it. It is not patina. A pet that changes colour in three seconds will never change colour in three years, because nothing is soaking in. A potter I buy from in Dingshu, the pottery town south of Yixing, put the difference to me in one line: 养壶养宠一个道理，急不得. Raising a pot and raising a pet are the same job. You cannot hurry it.

Why a toad, and why you feed it

The three-legged toad is the oldest money charm in the country. The story is Liu Hai, a Daoist immortal who tamed a toad that spat coins, and the toad has been squatting on shop counters and tea trays ever since, a coin in its mouth, bumps on its back. The rest of the bestiary works the same way: the pixiu that swallows wealth and refuses to let it out, the pig for sheer plenty, the elephant for steadiness, the crab on a lotus leaf because 和谐 sounds like harmony and a crab is never off balance.

Here is the part foreigners usually miss. Nobody prays to these things. There is no offering, no festival, no priest. You feed it. The verb is 养, to raise, and Chinese uses it for children, plants and animals alike. A tea pet is a small private contract: I keep pouring, you keep getting darker, and neither of us is in a hurry. On a bad afternoon it is also the only thing at the table that will accept whatever you spill.

Four rules, in order of how badly they hurt

One: buy unglazed clay or buy nothing. Zisha, duanni, qingshui ni, anything porous and honest. A plain zisha golden toad the size of a plum is the right first purchase: cheap enough that you will actually pour on it, ugly enough that you will not baby it. If you want a figure with more character, a purple clay Monkey King has deeper carving, and deep carving is what makes the high points of a patina legible years later.

Two: feed it the rinse and the dregs, not the good cups. The water you use to wake the leaves, and whatever is left in the fairness pitcher at the end, are enough. The film is the point; the expensive infusion is not. Pour, let it run, walk away.

Three: one tea family per pet. Same logic as one pot for one tea. A pet raised on ripe puer goes dark and picks up the cellar smell; keep pouring ripe puer and it stays coherent. Alternate ripe puer, jasmine and a green tea for a year and you get a grey object that smells of nothing in particular.

Four: never soak it, never soap it. A pet left standing in a bucket of tea grows mould in the pores, and mould does not come out, no matter what the internet tells you. Let it dry between sessions, brush it with a soft dry brush when the surface looks dull, and keep it away from detergent. If you want the trick version anyway, the heat-sensitive koi is a fine toy, as long as you know that is what you bought.

Wei's toad has one more thing I did not expect. The mouth is worn smooth on the inside, because for nine years he has been flicking the last drops from the pitcher into it, and clay gives way to a thumb.
