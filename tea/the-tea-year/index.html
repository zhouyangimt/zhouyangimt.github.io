The Tea Year: A Calendar for Buying Chinese Tea at the Right Moment

The second of April, a front room in Meijiawu with the door propped open. A gas wok sits in the corner, still warm. The farmer's wife is weighing leaves into fifty-gram portions on a brass scale, and the room smells of hot chestnut with something greener underneath. Three paper slips with prices written in ballpoint sit on the table, the top one marked 明前 in red.

A man in a leather jacket comes in and asks for the good stuff. She asks when he wants to drink it. He says a wedding, in October. She puts the top slip away and reaches for a tin at the back of the shelf. He protests. She does not look up: the April leaf will be flat by October, and she is not going to sell him a story.

Chinese tea is not a shelf you shop from. It is a calendar with two-week windows, and most of the money foreigners waste on tea is spent buying the right tea at the wrong time of year.

Why April tastes different

Through winter the bush sits still. Growth stops, the plant hoards nitrogen in its roots, and when the first warm days arrive in March the new shoots come slowly, fat with amino acids and low in the bitter catechins that sunlight builds later. Slow growth tastes sweet. Fast growth tastes strong.

That is the whole reason 明前 exists. Qingming, the tomb-sweeping festival, falls on 4 or 5 April. Leaves picked before it are the first flush: small, pale, and worth roughly twice what the same field yields ten days later. The second window closes at 谷雨, Grain Rain, around 19–20 April. Tea picked before that is 雨前. After Grain Rain the leaf gets bigger and the price drops again, which is why the two characters on the paper slip matter more than the name of the mountain.

The arithmetic behind those prices is brutal. A skilled picker needs around sixty thousand bud-and-leaf sets for one jin of dry Longjing, roughly five jin of fresh leaf to one jin finished, done by hand inside a two-week window on a hillside.

What you get for it is not intensity. It is the opposite. A good pre-Qingming Longjing brews a liquor so pale it looks like white grape juice, and the taste is closer to a smell than a flavour: cut grass, soy milk, a sweetness that arrives after you swallow and stays in the throat. Strong tea hits you. This kind of tea waits to be noticed.

The book that starts the year at 惊蛰

茶工作于惊蛰，尤以得天时为急。

Tea work begins at Awakening of Insects, and nothing is more urgent than catching heaven's timing. — Emperor Huizong, Treatise on Tea, 1107

Huizong was writing about the imperial tribute tea at Beiyuan in Fujian, steamed and pressed into cakes for the court. The plant has not changed its schedule since. Awakening of Insects falls around 5 March, and everything after that date is a race against the leaf getting coarser.

Summer belongs to flowers

By June the spring flush is finished and the good green tea is already in cold storage. What is happening in Fujian is jasmine. The flowers open at night, so they are picked at night, then layered with tea and left while the leaf drinks the scent; the spent blossom is sifted out and the round repeated. A high-grade jasmine goes through seven of these over about a month, which is why a good tin smells like a garden instead of a perfume bottle.

Most jasmine tea sold in the world skips all of that and gets sprayed with flavouring. The difference shows up in the cup: real scenting fades at the fourth or fifth infusion, spraying collapses at the second and leaves something flat and chemical behind.

Autumn is a second spring, with a different accent

Tieguanyin people have a four-character summary: 春水秋香, spring for the water, autumn for the fragrance. The autumn pick, after 秋分 in late September, gives thinner liquor and a louder aroma, and in Anxi that is the one many drinkers wait for.

In Yunnan the autumn pick around September and October is called 谷花茶, grain-flower tea, because it coincides with the rice flowering. It is cheaper than spring puer and less revered; for drinking young I often prefer it, rounder and easier to like in the first year. Fenghuang dancong has its own trick. After 霜降 in late October the bush pushes one last set of large thick leaves, picked into a tea called 雪片, snowflake: the loudest dancong of the year, all perfume, meant to be finished within twelve months.

Winter is for fire and waiting

Wuyi rock tea is picked at the end of April, and nobody drinks it in May. The leaf is roasted over charcoal in stages through the summer, then it has to rest, 退火, let the fire go out of it, for one to three months. The new crop is traditionally drunk after Mid-Autumn, five months or so after picking. A rougui roasted last week tastes like a campfire; the same tea in November tastes like fruit and stone.

What the calendar costs when you ignore it

Green tea is dead within a year of picking, and it dies faster in a kitchen. A tin of Longjing bought in December is either properly cold-stored old leaf, in which case it is fine but not what you paid for, or it is last spring's leaf kept in a warm room, in which case it tastes like hay. Neither is a scandal. Both are money spent on a season that has already left.

Spring tea also has a social function here that has nothing to do with flavour. The first 明前 Longjing of the year moves through offices and family groups in April, in small tins, hand-carried rather than shipped. Give someone last year's tea in April and you have said something you did not intend.

A year in six purchases

April. Green tea, and only green tea: Longjing, Biluochun, Anji Baicha, Taiping Houkui. Ask for the pick date, not the grade. Buy a hundred to two hundred grams per tea, seal it, and keep it in the fridge away from the door.

Late April to May. Spring black tea: Keemun, Jin Jun Mei, Dianhong. Black tea forgives a year on the shelf, so this is a buy-now-drink-later purchase.

June. Nothing. Save the money. The rock tea on the shelf was roasted last week and needs to rest; the puer cakes are still being pressed.

August. New jasmine from the summer scenting, and spring white tea, which has settled by now.

September to October. Autumn Tieguanyin, Yunnan 谷花, and this year's rock tea once it has rested past Mid-Autumn.

November to December. Dancong 雪片, and the last window for green tea you mean to drink before spring. After that, drink what you have.

Two rules cover most of it. Never buy a spring green in autumn unless you know where it was stored. And keep the quantities small enough to finish a bag before the next harvest arrives. Small enamel canisters holding a hundred grams are more use than a big tin you open every week; the storage guide has the longer version, and the six-bag list is what to put in them if you are starting from nothing.

The woman in Meijiawu was not being difficult. She was refusing to let a customer buy October's memory of April. Every tea on the shelf is a date pretending to be a product, and the date decides whether the money was well spent.

I still buy out of season sometimes. A rainy week in February, a tin with a pretty label, and I know exactly what I am doing and do it anyway. The difference is that I no longer expect it to taste like spring.
