Water: The Ingredient Nobody Prints on the Tin

By Zhouyang · Shanghai

In a teahouse ten minutes' walk from Hupao spring, on the edge of Hangzhou, the owner put two identical lidded bowls in front of me and made me taste both before he would say what I was drinking. Same Longjing, picked the same week in April, three grams in each. Same porcelain, same temperature, poured in the same second. The only difference was where the water in that kettle had come from.

The first cup was a photograph of Longjing. Green, vegetal, a little bitter at the edges, and then nothing at all once I swallowed. The second cup arrived in two parts. There was the sip, and then four or five seconds later something came back up the back of my throat like a door opening onto a warm room. Chestnut. Wet stone. Sweetness that did not come from sugar. 回甘, the taste that returns. I had been drinking that same tea at home for six years and had never once tasted it.

A brewed cup is more than 99 percent water. Three grams of leaf in a hundred and fifty millilitres, and everything that makes the stuff tea — caffeine, catechins, amino acids, aromatics — is a rounding error by mass. Water is not the solvent here. Water is the drink. Nobody prints it on the tin.

What Is Actually in Your Water

Four numbers decide what your tea tastes like. Total dissolved solids (TDS) counts everything dissolved in the water, which is mostly minerals. Hardness is the calcium and magnesium part of that number, and it is the part that hurts. Alkalinity, mostly bicarbonate, sets how stubbornly the water resists a change in pH. And chlorine or chloramine, added by your supplier on purpose, is what makes a cup taste like a swimming pool.

Hard water is the usual culprit. Calcium and magnesium ions bind to the polyphenols and aromatic compounds in the leaf and drag them out of solution as dull, heavy complexes. This is why black tea brewed in hard water looks muddy and sometimes leaves a film on the surface that Chinese drinkers call 茶锈, tea rust. Bicarbonate makes it worse on its own: above pH 8 the bright green of a Longjing liquor turns to a stewed yellow-brown, the catechins oxidise faster, and the cup tastes cooked rather than fresh.

The opposite mistake is softer than you expect. Distilled and reverse-osmosis water pull almost everything out, and the extraction stops working: without mineral content the leaf gives up less and the cup reads as empty. Chinese tea people have a one-character verdict for it, 薄. Thin. My own aim, after a few years of fiddling, is roughly 30 to 100 milligrams per litre of TDS, hardness below about 80, pH sitting between 6.5 and 7.5. Not pristine. Just enough.

Then there is the kettle. Water carries dissolved oxygen, part of why fresh water tastes alive, and boiling drives it off; hold a rolling boil for ten minutes and you have flat, tired 老水, old water. Lu Yu was listening to this in the eighth century. He named the three stages of a boil by their sound: 蟹眼, crab eyes, the first small bubbles; 鱼眼, fish eyes, a string of pearls; and 腾波鼓浪, billowing waves — by which point the water is old and no longer fit to drink. Modern version, no poetry required: boil once, hold the temperature, and stop topping up a kettle that has been boiling since breakfast.

Temperature is the last dial. Green and yellow tea want 75 to 85 degrees Celsius; white tea and light oolongs around 85 to 90; roasted oolongs and blacks 95 to 100; pu'er, both raw and ripe, gets the full boil. Under-temper a ripe pu'er and you get wet cardboard. Over-temper a spring green and you get bitterness with no aroma to carry it.

I spent thirteen years at Microsoft, Google and NVIDIA watching the same code behave differently on different machines, and I still needed a teahouse owner to pour two bowls for me before I understood it. The leaf is the code. The water is the runtime. You can buy perfect leaf and ship it onto a broken platform for a decade without noticing.

Water Is the Mother of Tea

The phrase on the wall of that teahouse was 水为茶之母，器为茶之父 — water is the mother of tea, the vessel is the father. It is not decoration. It is a claim about proportion.

Lu Yu's 茶经, the Classic of Tea, has a passage on water that reads like a field guide: 其水，用山水上，江水中，井水下. For water, mountain spring is best, river water next, well water worst. Then he goes narrower, and this is the line I keep coming back to. Take the spring that seeps from rock, or the water that moves slowly across a stone pool; do not drink the water of a rushing torrent. A man who spent his life on the road had opinions about flow rate.

The tradition never stopped measuring. The story goes that the Qianlong emperor carried a silver bucket around his empire, weighed spring waters against each other and ranked them with the lightest at the top, which is how a spring outside Beijing ended up with the title 天下第一泉. Today the echo is on every teahouse wall in Hangzhou: 龙井茶叶虎跑水, Longjing leaf and Hupao water, named as a pair because each is only half of the other.

What to Do About It

One: taste your water before you blame the tea. Drink a glass cold, then drink it warm. If it tastes of chlorine, plaster or chalk, you have found the problem without buying anything. Most people change tea three times before they consider changing the water underneath it. I did.

Two: spend fifteen dollars on a meter. A cheap TDS pen like this one turns an argument into a number in about four seconds. Under 50 and you are in thin territory; 50 to 150 is the comfortable middle for most tea; over 200 and you are fighting your own kitchen. My Shanghai tap runs a little over 200, which explains a great deal about my first six years of tea.

Three: filter for chlorine, but do not expect a filter to fix hardness. A carbon pitcher, like this Brita, removes chlorine, chloramine and some metals, and that alone will noticeably clean up a delicate green tea. It will barely touch calcium and magnesium. If your water is genuinely hard, the honest answer is low-mineral bottled water.

Four: boil once and hold. A variable-temperature kettle is the single best piece of tea gear after the gaiwan itself, because it lets you stop guessing at 80 degrees. Boil the water, set it to the temperature your tea wants, and pour. Do not reheat the same water all afternoon, and do not top up a hot kettle with cold water. That is how you end up brewing with a mix that has been boiled four times.

Five: ignore anything sold as water for tea. The label tells you everything: TDS, hardness, sometimes pH. If it does not, the bottle is selling you a photograph of a mountain.
