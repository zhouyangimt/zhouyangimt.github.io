The Pot You Never Wash: What a Yixing Teapot Actually Absorbs

Everything in my kitchen gets washed. The wok goes into hot water before dinner is over. The cast iron gets oiled. The knives get dried and put away with the blades facing left. One object never touches soap, and it doesn't live in the kitchen at all: a 200 ml 西施壶 that has been sitting on my desk for eleven years, bought in 2013 from a shop in 丁蜀镇, the little town outside Yixing where the clay comes out of the ground.

Here is everything I do with it. Hot water in, swirl, out. Hot water over the outside. Turn it upside down on a folded cloth with the lid off and leave it there. Forty seconds, most of which is waiting for the kettle.

About a year into owning it I poured boiling water in and the steam came back smelling of something I hadn't brewed since spring. Not tea exactly. Closer to the inside of an old wooden drawer. I stood there with the pot in one hand and understood that I wasn't holding a cup with a lid. I was holding a sponge that remembers what I've been brewing, and everything about looking after one follows from that.

It is also the only piece of equipment I own that gets worse the better you clean it. Thirteen years of writing software taught me that grime is always a bug. This is the exception, and it took me a decade to stop fighting it.

What the clay actually is

紫砂, “purple sand”, is not a mineral and not a single material. It's a family of iron-rich stoneware clays dug around 丁蜀 in southern Jiangsu — 紫泥, 红泥, 段泥 — and the iron is what colours them. Hematite in the grain is what gives a fired pot that brown-purple depth no glaze imitates well.

They're fired somewhere around 1,150 to 1,200°C. Hot enough to sinter the clay into a solid body, not hot enough to melt it into glass. Porcelain finishes its firing vitrified, with water absorption well under one per cent. Yixing stays open, at roughly two.

Two per cent sounds like a rounding error. It's the whole design. 文震亨 wrote it down in the 1630s in 《长物志》: 茶壶以砂者为上，盖既不夺香，又无熟汤气 — a pot of sand-clay is best, because it neither steals the fragrance nor gives the water a cooked taste. He had no instrument for measuring porosity. He was describing an open body that keeps the aromatics a glazed pot would bounce straight back at you, and lets the leaf breathe instead of stewing it.

包浆 is a receipt, not a coat

The shine people chase has a name, 包浆, and it is three things arriving slowly at once: tea solids — tannins, polysaccharides, minerals — drying inside the surface pores; the oil from your hands, because you touch a pot far more than you notice; and the polish of a cloth, week after week. Nothing is applied to the pot. No oil, no wax, no paste.

Over a few years the film fills the micro-scratches left by the potter's tools. A rough surface scatters light in every direction; a filled one doesn't. So the pot stops looking dusty and starts looking lit from underneath. Potters talk about it the way guitar players talk about the back of a neck going dark where the hand lives.

The shine is the least interesting part. What the clay keeps is smell. Every session leaves aroma compounds in those pores and they don't leave. Pour boiling water into a pot that has spent a year on one oolong and what comes up is not neutral steam: wet stone, old paper, something sweet underneath. The pot is empty and it is still answering.

This is the same bargain as a wine barrel or a cast-iron pan, with one difference. A pan's seasoning is fat polymerised by heat into a hard layer. This is cooler chemistry — adsorption, not polymerisation — and it comes off if you attack it with the wrong thing. Which is why the care instructions are almost entirely a list of things not to do.

A town, a tribute tea, and a rule with no paper trail

Yixing used to be called 阳羡. Its tea was tribute in the Tang, and 卢仝 wrote the line Chinese tea drinkers still recite: 天子须尝阳羡茶，百草不敢先开花 — the Son of Heaven must taste Yangxian tea; the hundred grasses dare not flower first. The pots came much later. Tradition credits 供春, a servant at 金沙寺 during the 正德 reign of 1506 to 1521, with the first vessel made as a teapot rather than a jar. 时大彬 turned that into a profession a century on. By the 1640s 周高起 could write, in 《阳羡茗壶系》: 壶经用久，涤拭日加，自发暗然之光，入手可鉴 — a pot used long, rinsed and wiped daily, gives off a dim lustre of its own; take it in your hand and it reflects you.

Read that sentence again for what it credits: use, rinsing, wiping. Not oil, not a secret, not a product. In 2006 the 宜兴紫砂陶制作技艺 went onto the first national list of intangible cultural heritage, and the doctrine in the paperwork is still the 1640s one.

The rule everybody repeats is 一壶不事二茶 — one pot serves one tea. It's in every tea shop in the country. I have gone through the Ming and Qing tea books on my shelf and I can't find it in any of them. 许次纾's 《茶疏》 from 1597 has something adjacent and different: 一壶之茶，只堪再巡 — one pot of tea is good for about two rounds — which is about how many infusions a leaf gives, not about dedicating a pot for life. As far as I can tell, the strict version is a collector's rule from the twentieth century, and it only makes sense once a pot has been in use long enough to hold something worth protecting.

The physics is real even where the pedigree isn't. A pot that has brewed 铁观音 and then 熟普 does both worse, because the clay doesn't forget. But that's advice about a seasoned pot, not a law about clay. A new pot, a cheap pot, a pot you haven't decided about yet — brew whatever you like in it and find out what it does.

How to keep one alive

Wash it with hot water and nothing else. No soap, no dishwasher, no vinegar soak. The surfactants and the fragrance compounds in dish soap are small molecules, and they go into exactly the same pores that hold your tea. You will not rinse them out. I have tasted a soaped pot. It tastes of soap for months.

Rinse it while it's still hot, then dry it upside down with the lid off, on a cloth rather than on stone. Wet clay sealed inside a closed pot grows a smell that no amount of tea will cover. Leave the lid beside it, not on it.

Don't shock it. 朱泥 shrinks more in the kiln than 紫泥 and rings higher when you tap it; it will crack if you pour boiling water into a cold pot in February. Give it a splash of hot water inside and out first and let it come up to temperature, the same reason the kettle matters more than most people think.

Pick one tea, eventually. Not on day one. If you want a starting point, a roasted oolong leaves a warmer, breadier residue than a green tea and forgives a pot that isn't seasoned yet — this Shui Xian is the sort of leaf that makes the whole exercise worth it, and there's a longer piece on it here.

Opening a new pot: skip the tofu and sugarcane boil. It does nothing chemically — 豆腐 doesn't draw anything out of fired clay that hot water can't. What does matter is kiln dust and wax. Cheap pots get polished with wax, sometimes worse, to fake ten years of patina. Pour boiling water over a new pot and smell it. Wet earth is right. Furniture polish means send it back.

The woman who sold me that first pot in 丁蜀镇 gave me one instruction and no product: 壶是养出来的，不是擦出来的. You don't polish it into shape. You raise it. Eleven years on, the pot smells of 水仙 and the desk smells of nothing, which is how it should be.

What I like about the whole arrangement is that the correct behaviour is mostly abstention. No cream, no brush, no ritual worth photographing. In a hobby built on gear, the most valuable object in the room turns out to be the one you're told not to clean properly.
